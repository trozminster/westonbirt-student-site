Cancelling After Factorising

An algebraic fraction looks like it should simplify the same way a numerical one does - but cancelling only ever works with factors, never with individual terms of a sum, so a fraction like x+3x has to stay exactly as it is until there's something to factorise.

By the end you’ll know…
  • An algebraic fraction cancels only by common factors, never by individual terms of a sum.
  • A numerator or denominator that is a sum must be factorised before anything can cancel.
  • Cancelling a bracket that appears in both leaves 1 in its place, not nothing.
  • An even number is 2n and an odd number 2n + 1, so statements about factors and multiples can be proved by factorising the general expression.

Cancelling only works with factors

Cancel only a factor of the whole top and the whole bottom. In x+3x, the x is only part of a sum. So nothing cancels.

A fraction simplifies by cancelling a factor that's common to both the numerator and the denominator - dividing top and bottom by the same thing. This only works when that common thing is a factor of the WHOLE numerator and the WHOLE denominator, not just part of a sum. In x+3x, the x in the denominator is not a factor of the numerator x+3 (x+3 doesn't divide evenly by x), so nothing cancels, even though an x appears in both - cancelling the x's here would be as wrong as saying 2+32 simplifies to 3.

Factorise first, then cancel

Factorise a sum before cancelling. x²+3xx = x(x+3)x = x+3. A bracket cancelled top and bottom leaves 1, not 0.

When the numerator or denominator (or both) is a sum, it must be factorised into a product before anything can be cancelled - turning the sum into factors is what makes a common factor visible. For example, x²+3xx has a numerator that factorises to x(x+3), so it becomes x(x+3)x, and now the x is a genuine common factor of the whole numerator and the whole denominator, cancelling to leave x+31 = x+3. If a bracket appears identically in both the numerator and the denominator once factorised, cancelling it leaves 1 in its place there, not nothing - a fraction can't lose a part of itself down to zero.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Representing even and odd numbers algebraically

Any even number is 2n, where n is a whole number. Any odd number is 2n+1. Algebra then proves a claim for every n, not just examples.

Any even number can be written as 2n, where n is a whole number, because an even number is exactly n lots of 2 - it's a multiple of 2 by definition. Any odd number can be written as 2n+1 (one more than an even number). Writing numbers this way, using a letter for 'any whole number', turns a specific numerical claim into a general one that can be proved algebraically rather than just checked on a few examples.

Proving with factorised expressions

Write the numbers algebraically, combine them, then factorise. A factor outside the bracket proves the claim for every n.

To decide whether a statement like 'the sum of two consecutive even numbers is always a multiple of 4' is true or false, write the numbers algebraically (2n and 2n+2), combine them as the statement describes, and factorise the result. If the factorised form has the required factor (here, 4) sitting outside the bracket, the statement is proved for every possible value of n, not just the ones tried - factorising is what turns 'it worked for these examples' into 'it will always work'.
PROOF: CONSECUTIVE ODDS
Prove that the sum of any two consecutive odd numbers is always a multiple of 4.
Write two consecutive odds2n + 1 and 2n + 3 The next odd number is two more, since odd numbers are 2 apart.
Add them(2n + 1) + (2n + 3) = 4n + 4
Factorise= 4(n + 1)
4(n + 1) is 4 times a whole number, so it is always a multiple of 4. This holds for every whole number value of n, which proves the statement.
EVEN TIMES ODD
Now prove that the product of any even number and any odd number is always even.
Write even and odd2n and 2m + 1 A different letter, since the two numbers need not be related.
Multiply them2n(2m + 1) = 4mn + 2n
Factorise= 2(2mn + n)
2(2mn + n) is 2 times a whole number, so it is always even. This holds for every whole number value of n and m, which proves the statement.
