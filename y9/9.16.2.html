The And Rule

When two events are independent, their combined probability follows a simple rule: multiply. Today is about recognising an "and" situation and applying that rule correctly.

By the end you’ll know…
  • For two independent events, P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B).
  • Multiplying makes the answer smaller, which fits: both happening is less likely than either alone.
  • The rule extends to more than two independent events by multiplying all of their probabilities.
  • In set notation 'A and B' is the intersection, A ∩ B.

The and-rule

For two independent events, P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B). Multiplying makes the answer smaller, which fits: both happening is less likely than either alone.

More than two events

The rule extends to more than two independent events by multiplying all of their probabilities. In set notation "A and B" is the intersection, A ∩ B.

Extending to three or more

The and-rule extends to any number of independent events: multiply every probability together. Each extra event makes the combined probability smaller still.

Set notation in practice

An "and" in the question signals the intersection, A ∩ B. It usually means multiply.

A ∩ B is read directly from a description using "and". Recognising the word "and" is usually the quickest way to spot which rule a question needs.
