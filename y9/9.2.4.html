Topic 9.2 · Linear Graphs and y = mx + c · Lesson 5 of 7

Discovering y = mx + c

So far you've drawn lines from a table of values, one point at a time. But every straight line's equation, written as y=mx+c, already contains everything you need to sketch it instantly, without a table at all - m tells you how steep it is and which way it leans, and c tells you exactly where it starts.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In y=mx+c, m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept.
  • The y-intercept is where the line crosses the y-axis, which is the value of y when x=0
  • A line can be sketched from its equation alone: mark c on the y-axis, then step out using the gradient.
  • Parallel lines have the same gradient, so their equations share the same m and differ only in c.
  • A negative m gives a line falling from left to right.

What m and c are

In y=mx+c, m is the gradient. c is the y-intercept, where the line crosses the y-axis.

Any linear equation written in the form y=mx+c has two useful numbers built into it: m is the gradient of the line, and c is the y-intercept - the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis, which is the value of y when x=0

Sketching from the equation

Sketch a line without a table: mark c on the y-axis first. Then step m up (down if negative) for every 1 across.

A line can be sketched directly from its equation, without a table of values: mark c on the y-axis first, then use m to step out further points - m units up (or down, if negative) for every 1 unit across.

Positive and negative m

A positive m rises from left to right. A negative m falls from left to right.

A positive m gives a line rising from left to right; a negative m gives a line falling from left to right.

Learn

Reading the equation off a graph

Read c where the line crosses the y-axis. Measure m as rise ÷ run between two points. Then write y=mx+c.

Reading a graph in reverse gives its equation: the y-intercept is read directly from where the line crosses the y-axis, and the gradient is found by measuring vertical change over horizontal change between two points on the line, exactly as before. Once both are known, the equation can be written as y=mx+c.

Parallel lines

Parallel lines share the same m. Their c values differ.

Two lines are parallel exactly when they share the same value of m - the same steepness and direction - even though they cross the y-axis at different points, so they have different values of c.
