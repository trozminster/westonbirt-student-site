Topic 9.2 · Linear Graphs and y = mx + c · Lesson 7 of 7

Linear Graphs in Context

A graph is never just lines and numbers when it's telling a real story - a distance-time graph's steepness is somebody's speed, and a phone bill's graph has a fixed line-rental hiding in its intercept. Today you'll learn to read the meaning behind the numbers, not just the numbers themselves.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In a real context both axes carry units, so the gradient carries units too: y units per x unit.
  • On a distance-time graph the gradient is the speed.
  • The intercept is the value when the x variable is zero, such as a fixed charge before any usage.
  • A straight line means a constant rate of change, which is what makes the model linear.

Gradient as a rate

On a real-world graph, the gradient is a rate: y-units per x-unit. On a distance-time graph, the gradient is speed.

In a real-world graph, both axes represent actual quantities with units, so the gradient is not just a bare number but a rate: y-units per x-unit. On a distance-time graph, where the vertical axis is distance and the horizontal axis is time, the gradient represents speed - distance travelled per unit of time.

A straight line means a constant rate

A straight line means the rate is constant. On a distance-time graph, that means a steady speed.

A straight line on such a graph means the rate of change is constant throughout - the object is travelling at exactly the same speed the whole time, which is exactly what makes the graph linear rather than curved.

What the intercept means in context

The intercept is the y-value when x is zero. It is often a starting amount or fixed charge.

In context, the y-intercept is the value of the y-variable when the x-variable is zero - often a starting amount or a fixed charge that applies before any of the x-variable has been used, rather than the total. Interpreting a graph means turning both numbers into a sentence about the real situation: stating what the gradient's rate means and what the intercept's starting value means, not just calculating them.

Saying it as a sentence

Describe the gradient as a rate, with its units. Describe the intercept as the value at the start.

To interpret a graph fully: read the gradient with its units and describe it as a rate ('the cost increases by (rate) for every (unit) used'); read the intercept and describe it as the value at the start ('the fixed cost before any use').
WORKED EXAMPLE C
A graph shows the cost of a phone plan
in pounds against the number of minutes used, as a straight line from (0,15) to (100,35). Find the gradient and explain what it means.
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Using the same graph, find the intercept and explain what it means.
YOUR TURN · e
A graph shows the cost of hiring a bike in pounds against the number of hours hired, as a straight line from (0,5) to (4,17). Find the gradient and intercept, and write a sentence explaining what each one means in this context. (Hint: the gradient's sentence should include units of pounds per hour; the intercept's sentence should describe a fixed charge, not a total.)
gradient =(17−5)÷(4−0) =3, so the hire costs £3 for every hour. Intercept =5, so there is a fixed £5 charge before any hours are counted.
YOUR TURN · f
A graph shows the amount of water in a tank in litres against time in minutes, as a straight line from (0,200) to (10,150), the tank is draining, so this line falls. Find the gradient and intercept, and write a sentence explaining what each one means, including why the gradient is negative in this context.
gradient =(150−200)÷(10−0) =−5, so the tank loses 5 litres every minute; it is negative because the water is draining, not filling. Intercept =200, the litres in the tank at the start.
