Topic 9.1 · Prime Factors, HCF and LCM · Lesson 2 of 4

What Prime Factors Reveal

You can tell instantly whether a number is a perfect square or a perfect cube just by looking at its prime factorisation - no square-rooting or cube-rooting needed. That same idea tells you exactly what to multiply by to turn any number into the nearest square or cube.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A square number has only even powers in its prime factorisation, because squaring doubles every power.
  • A cube number has only powers that are multiples of three, for the same reason.
  • The smallest multiplier making a number square is the product of the primes whose powers are odd.
  • The smallest multiplier making it a cube brings every power up to the next multiple of three.

Why squares and cubes have special prime factorisations

Squaring a number doubles every power in its prime factorisation. So a square number has every power even. A cube number has every power a multiple of three.

Squaring a number doubles every power in its prime factorisation, because squaring means multiplying the number by itself, which doubles the count of every prime factor. So a square number's prime factorisation has every power even. Cubing multiplies every power by three, so a cube number's prime factorisation has every power a multiple of three (0, 3, 6, 9, ...).

Checking square or cube from the factorisation

A number is square if every power is even. It is a cube if every power is a multiple of three.

To test whether a number is square, write its prime factorisation and check every power is even. To test whether it's a cube, check every power is a multiple of three. A power of zero (a prime not present at all) counts as both even and a multiple of three, so it never blocks either test.

Making a number square

An even power needs nothing more. Multiply by each prime with an odd power, once each.

If a prime's power in the factorisation is already even, it doesn't need any more of that prime. If a prime's power is odd, multiplying by one more of that prime makes its power even. So the smallest number to multiply by to make the original a square number is the product of just the primes whose powers are odd (each to the power 1).

Making a number cube

A power already a multiple of three needs nothing. Otherwise raise it to the NEXT multiple of three, not up from zero.

If a prime's power is already a multiple of three, it needs nothing more. If it isn't, multiply by enough of that prime to bring its power up to the NEXT multiple of three above its current power - not up to three from zero, but up from wherever the power currently is.
