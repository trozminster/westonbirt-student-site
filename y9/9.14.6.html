Substitution Method

Sometimes one equation already hands you an expression for a variable. Today is about using that expression directly, instead of eliminating, to collapse a pair of equations into one.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Where one equation already gives a variable on its own, that expression can replace the variable in the other equation.
  • The result is a single equation in one unknown.
  • The substituted expression is bracketed, so that any multiplication reaches all of it.
  • Substitution suits a pair where one equation is already rearranged; elimination suits a pair where neither is.

Replacing a variable

One equation may already give a variable on its own, like y = 2x. Replace that variable in the other equation. That leaves one equation in one unknown.

Where one equation already gives a variable on its own, that expression can replace the variable in the other equation. The result is a single equation in one unknown.

Bracket the substitution

Always bracket the substituted expression. A multiplier in front must reach all of it.

The substituted expression is bracketed, so that any multiplication reaches all of it. Substitution suits a pair where one equation is already rearranged; elimination suits a pair where neither is.
DIRECT SUBSTITUTION
Solve y = 2x + 1 and 3x + y = 16 by substitution.
Write the equationsy = 2x + 1 (1)
3x + y = 16 (2)
Substitute (1) into (2)3x + (2x + 1) = 16
Collect like terms5x + 1 = 16
Subtract 1 from both sides5x = 15
Divide by 5x = 3
Substitute into (1)y = 2 × 3 + 1
= 7
x = 3
y = 7

2x + (x + 5) = 17, 3x = 12, x = 4, y = 9

Solve y = 3x - 2 and x + y = 6 by substitution.
x + (3x - 2) = 6, 4x = 8, x = 2, y = 4

Rearranging first

When neither equation gives a variable alone, rearrange one first. Then substitute as before.

When neither equation gives a variable alone, one equation can be rearranged into that form before substitution begins, turning it into a substitution pair.

Choosing the method

Substitution and elimination always reach the same answer. Choose whichever needs less arithmetic for the pair.

Substitution and elimination always reach the same answer; the choice is about which involves less arithmetic for that particular pair.
REARRANGE, THEN SUBSTITUTE
Solve x + 2y = 11 and 3x - y = 5 by substitution.
Write the equationsx + 2y = 11 (1)
3x - y = 5 (2)
Rearrange (1)x = 11 - 2y (3)
Substitute (3) into (2)3(11 - 2y) - y = 5
Expand the bracket33 - 6y - y = 5
Collect like terms33 - 7y = 5
Subtract 33 from both sides-7y = -28
Divide by -7y = 4
Substitute into (3)x = 11 - 2 × 4
= 3
x = 3
y = 4

Explain why a substituted expression must be bracketed before it is combined with other terms.
Without brackets, a multiplication in front of the substituted expression only reaches the first term of it, giving a different and wrong equation.
