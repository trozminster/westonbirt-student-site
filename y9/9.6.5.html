Topic 9.6 · Right-Angled Trigonometry · Lesson 5 of 5

Mixed Problems in Context

Every triangle so far has already been drawn for you. Real trigonometry problems - a plane's height, a ship's distance from a cliff, the diagonal of a box - start as words, and the first job is always to turn those words into a labelled right-angled triangle.

By the end you’ll know…
  • An angle of elevation is measured upward from the horizontal, and an angle of depression downward from it.
  • The elevation and depression between the same two points are equal, because the two horizontals are parallel.
  • A worded problem is turned into a labelled right-angled triangle before any ratio is chosen.
  • In a three-dimensional solid the problem is solved by finding a right-angled triangle inside it and redrawing that triangle on its own.
  • A multi-step problem may need Pythagoras for one length and trigonometry for another, with exact values kept between the steps.

Elevation and depression

Elevation is measured upward from the horizontal and depression downward. Between the same two points the two angles are equal, as alternate angles.

The angle of elevation is the angle measured upward from the horizontal to a point above - for example, from a person on the ground looking up at the top of a building. The angle of depression is the angle measured downward from the horizontal to a point below - for example, from someone at the top of the building looking down at the person on the ground. Both angles are always measured from the horizontal, never from the ground or from a wall. Because the horizontal line at the person's eye level and the horizontal line at the top of the building are parallel, the angle of elevation from the ground to the top and the angle of depression from the top to the ground are equal - they are alternate angles between two parallel lines.

From words to triangle

Sketch a right-angled triangle from the words first. Put the right angle at the foot of whatever is vertical. Label each side before choosing a ratio.

A worded problem gives clues, not a picture: a height, a horizontal distance, or an angle, plus a request for one of the others. The first step is always to sketch a right-angled triangle, mark the right angle at the base of whatever is vertical (a tower, a cliff, a tree), and label each part with what the words tell you - which side is opposite the angle, which is adjacent, and where the hypotenuse is. Only once the triangle is drawn and labelled does the choice of ratio become the next step, exactly as in previous lessons.

Multi-step problems

Some problems chain Pythagoras and trigonometry. Use the unrounded first answer in the second step.

Keep the exact value. Some problems need two calculations chained together - Pythagoras to find a length, then trigonometry using that length, or trigonometry to find a length, then Pythagoras using that length. Whichever comes second must use the unrounded result of the first step, kept on the calculator or written with several extra decimal places, not the value already rounded to 3 significant figures - rounding early builds an error into the next step that shows up in the final answer.

Extending into three dimensions

A solid holds many right-angled triangles. Redraw the one joining the two points as a flat triangle. Then solve it with Pythagoras or trigonometry.

A cuboid, prism or pyramid contains many right-angled triangles, but only one of them connects the two points a question actually asks about. The method is always the same: find that specific triangle inside the solid, redraw it as a flat, separate 2D diagram exactly as in every earlier lesson, then solve it with Pythagoras and/or trigonometry as usual. Working directly inside the 3D drawing, without ever redrawing the flat triangle, is where most mistakes happen, because it's easy to misjudge which lengths in the solid are actually part of the triangle being solved.
