Expanding Double Brackets

A single bracket outside a shape's dimensions turns a sketch into an expression in seconds - but two brackets multiplied together, like the sides of a rectangle both written in terms of x, need a more careful method: every term in the first must meet every term in the second before anything gets simplified.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Expanding double brackets multiplies every term in the first bracket by every term in the second, giving four products before simplifying.
  • (x+a)2 means (x+a)(x+a), so it expands to x2 +2ax +a2, not x2+a2
  • Several single brackets are expanded one at a time, then simplified by collecting like terms.
  • A negative outside a bracket changes the sign of every term inside it.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Expanding a single bracket

Expanding multiplies every term inside the bracket by the term outside. So 3(2x+5) =6x+15
A negative outside multiplies every term too: −2(3x−4) =−6x+8

Expanding a bracket means multiplying every term inside it by the term (or number) outside. 3(2x+5) means 3 lots of (2x+5): multiply both 2x and 5 by 3, giving 6x+15
If the term outside is negative, e.g. −2(3x−4), the negative multiplies every term inside too: −2×3x =−6x, and −2×−4=+8, giving −6x+8 - the sign of the second term flips because a negative times a negative is positive.

Several brackets in one expression

Expand each bracket separately, keeping the sign in front of it. Then collect like terms to simplify.

An expression can contain more than one bracket, sometimes with a plus or minus between them. Expand each bracket separately, keeping track of any sign in front of it, then collect like terms (the x terms together, the number terms together) to simplify. This is exactly what's needed in geometric contexts: if a shape's sides are given as algebraic expressions, its perimeter is the sum of those sides, and its area a product of them - both may need expanding before they can be simplified.

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Expanding double brackets

In (x+3)(x+5), every term in one bracket multiplies every term in the other. That gives four products: x2 +5x +3x +15 =x2+8x+15

Two brackets multiplied together, like (x+3)(x+5), mean every term in the first bracket must multiply every term in the second - four products in total before anything is simplified. The grid shows them: x and +5 across the top, x and +3 down the side, and each cell is its row times its column - x×x=x2, x×5=5x, 3×x=3x and 3×5=15
Adding these: x2 +5x +3x +15, which simplifies (collecting the two x terms) to x2+8x+15

The squared bracket (x+a)2

(x+a)2 means (x+a)(x+a), never x2+a2
The two identical brackets give ax twice, so the middle term doubles to 2ax.

(x+a)2 means (x+a)(x+a), not x2+a2 - it must be expanded as a full double bracket. In the grid, every term of one bracket multiplies every term of the other: x×x=x2, x×a=ax, a×x=ax and a×a=a2
Adding: x2 +ax +ax +a2 =x2 +2ax +a2 - the middle term appears twice because the two brackets are identical, so it doubles rather than cancelling.

Explain why expanding a double bracket like (x+4)(x+6) gives four products, and why (x+a)2 is not the same as x2+a2
Double-bracket expansion multiplies every term in the first bracket by every term in the second, giving 2×2=4 products; (x+a)2 also has a cross-term 2ax that x2+a2 misses.
