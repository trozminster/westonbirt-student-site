Reverse Percentage Problems

Two of the most common real-world percentage problems - working out a price before VAT was added, and working out a price before a sale discount was applied - are both reverse percentage problems in disguise. This lesson applies the reverse method to these contexts, and then sharpens your eye for spotting which type of problem you're facing before you start calculating, since picking the wrong method is the single most common mistake here.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In a reverse percentage problem the amount given is the result of the change, not the original.
  • The original is found by dividing by the multiplier, never by applying the percentage to the amount given.
  • A price including 20% VAT is 120% of the pre-VAT price, so dividing by 1.2 recovers it.
  • A sale price after a 30% reduction is 70% of the original, so dividing by 0.7 recovers it.
  • The wording tells which is which: 'before' or 'original' signals a reverse problem, 'after' an ordinary one.

VAT as a reverse percentage problem

A price including 20% VAT is 120% of the pre-VAT price. So divide it by 1.2 to find the price before VAT.

VAT (Value Added Tax) is added on top of a pre-VAT price, so a price including 20% VAT is 120% of the original pre-VAT price - the multiplier is 1.2
If you're given the price INCLUDING VAT and asked for the price BEFORE VAT, that given price is the new amount, not the original, so you divide by 1.2 to recover the original pre-VAT price.

Sale prices as a reverse percentage problem

A price reduced by 30% is 70% of the original, so the multiplier is 0.7
Divide the sale price by 0.7, never by 0.3

A sale price after a reduction works the same way. A price reduced by 30% in a sale is 70% of the original full price, so the multiplier is 0.7
Given the sale price and asked for the original full price, divide the sale price by 0.7 - never by 0.3, since 0.3 is only the part that was taken away, not the multiplier connecting the two prices. In both VAT and sale-price problems, the amount you are given is always the result of the change, so it is always the new amount to be divided, never the original to be multiplied.

Reading the wording to identify problem type

Given the original and asked for the result, multiply by the multiplier. Given the result and asked for the original, divide by it.

Every percentage problem gives you two amounts (or asks you to find one) connected by a percentage change, but which amount you're given determines the method. If you're given the ORIGINAL amount and asked for the result, it's an ordinary change: multiply by the multiplier. If you're given the RESULT and asked for the original, it's a reverse problem: divide by the multiplier. Certain words are strong clues: "before", "original", and "pre-VAT" signal you've been given the result and must reverse; "find the new price" or "what is it now" signal you've been given the original and should apply the change forwards. "After" can sit on either side: "after a 10% rise it costs £33" gives you the result.

Why misreading the direction is the most common error

Ordinary and reverse problems use the same multiplier. The error is choosing whether to multiply or divide. Decide from what you HAVE and what you WANT.

Both ordinary and reverse problems use exactly the same multiplier - the error isn't in building the multiplier, it's in choosing whether to multiply or divide by it. A quick habit that catches most mistakes: read the question and identify in words which amount you HAVE and which you WANT, before writing any numbers down; then decide multiply (original → result) or divide (result → original) based on that, not based on which operation "feels right" for the numbers involved.
