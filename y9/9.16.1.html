Types of Events

Not every pair of events behaves the same way. Today sorts events into three kinds – independent, dependent, and mutually exclusive – since which kind a pair is decides which rule applies to it later.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Two events are independent when one happening does not change the probability of the other.
  • They are dependent when it does, as when an item is taken and not replaced.
  • They are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen at once.
  • Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent: mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent, since one occurring makes the other impossible.
  • The probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1, so P(not A)=1−P(A).

Three relationships

Independent: one event doesn't change the other's probability. Dependent: it does, as when items aren't replaced. Mutually exclusive: they can't both happen at once.

Two events are independent when one happening does not change the probability of the other. They are dependent when it does, as when an item is taken and not replaced. They are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen at once.

Mutually exclusive is not independent

Mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent. One happening makes the other's probability zero.

Mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent, since one occurring makes the other impossible – its probability drops to zero. The probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1, so P(not A)=1−P(A).

The complement rule

All the outcome probabilities sum to 1
So P(not A)=1−P(A). Use it when not A is easier to count.

The probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1, so P(not A)=1−P(A). This is quick whenever "not A" is easier to count than A itself.

Telling the three apart

Independence asks: does one event change the other's probability? Mutual exclusivity asks: can both happen on the same trial? These are separate questions.

Independent asks whether one event changes the other's probability. Dependent is when it does. Mutually exclusive asks whether both can happen together on the same trial – a separate question from independence entirely.
