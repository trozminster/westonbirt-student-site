Topic 9.5 · Reverse Percentages and Compound Interest · Lesson 5 of 7

Simple and Compound Interest

Interest is the reward for saving money (or the cost of borrowing it), and banks calculate it in two different ways. Simple interest pays the same amount every year; compound interest pays interest on the interest already earned, so it grows faster. Telling them apart is the difference between a growing savings account and a spiralling debt.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Simple interest adds the same amount each period, always calculated on the original.
  • Over n periods simple interest totals n times the interest for one period.
  • Compound interest applies the multiplier to the running total, so each period earns interest on the interest already added.
  • After n periods the compound amount is the original x multiplierⁿ.
  • Compound growth therefore accelerates, and beyond the first period always exceeds simple interest at the same rate.

Simple interest adds a fixed amount each period

Simple interest is found on the original amount only. The same interest is added every period. e.g. £200 at 5% earns £10 every year.

Simple interest is calculated once, on the original amount only, and that same amount of interest is added every period (usually a year) - it never grows or shrinks between periods. One period's interest is found using the multiplier method from Lesson 9.5.1: original × rate as a decimal. Over n periods, the total simple interest is n times that one-period amount, since the same interest repeats unchanged each time.

Finding the total amount after simple interest

Total = original + n × one period's interest. Interest already earned never earns more interest.

To find the total amount after n years of simple interest, add the total interest (n periods × one period's interest) to the original amount. Because simple interest never touches the interest already earned, this total can also be found directly: original + (original × rate × n), or equivalently original × (1 + rate × n).

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Compound interest applies the multiplier to the running total

Compound interest is earned on the running total, not the original. Apply the same multiplier again each period.

Unlike simple interest, compound interest is calculated on the running total, not just the original amount - so each period, interest is earned on the interest already added, not just on the starting amount. This means compound interest uses the multiplier method from Lesson 9.5.1 (and 9.5.2), applying the same multiplier again and again to the amount reached so far, rather than adding a fixed amount each time.

The power shortcut

Applying a multiplier n times is the same as multiplierⁿ. So after n years, total = original × multiplierⁿ.

Applying the same multiplier n times in a row (multiplier × multiplier × multiplier × ... n times) is the same as raising the multiplier to the power n. So after n years of compound interest, the total amount is: original × multiplierⁿ. This power shortcut avoids calculating year by year - build the one-year multiplier once, raise it to the power of however many years, then multiply by the original amount in a single calculation.
