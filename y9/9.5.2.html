Successive Percentage Changes

Real situations often apply two percentage changes one after another - a pay rise followed by a bonus, a price hike followed by a sale. You might expect the two percentages to simply add or cancel, but multipliers don't work that way. This lesson shows how to combine successive changes into a single multiplier, and why the order never matters, even though the total effect rarely matches your gut instinct.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Successive percentage changes multiply their multipliers together.
  • Two changes therefore combine into a single multiplier, applied once.
  • The combined effect is not the sum of the percentages: a 10% rise then a 10% fall is x1.1×0.9=x0.99, not a return to the original.
  • The order the changes are applied in does not matter, since multiplication can be done in any order.

Combining successive multipliers

Two percentage changes in turn are two multipliers in turn. Multiply them for one combined multiplier. A 10% rise then a 20% rise is 1.1×1.2=1.32

When an amount is changed by one percentage and then changed again by a second percentage, each change is its own multiplier, and the overall effect is the original amount multiplied by both multipliers in turn. Because multiplication can be applied one factor at a time, this is the same as multiplying the two multipliers together first, then applying that single combined multiplier once. A 10% rise followed by a 20% rise is 1.1×1.2=1.32, a single 32% rise overall.

Why the percentages don't just add

A 10% rise then a 10% fall is × 0.99: a 1% fall. The second percentage is of a different amount. The order of the changes doesn't matter.

It's tempting to add 10% and 10% to get a 20% change, but a 10% rise then a 10% fall combine to 1.1×0.9=0.99, a 1% fall overall - not a return to the original amount. This happens because the second change is applied to the result of the first, which is a different amount from the original, so the two percentages are percentages of different amounts and cannot simply cancel. The order of the two changes does not affect the final combined multiplier, since 1.1×0.9=0.9×1.1 - multiplication in any order gives the same product.

Spotting successive changes in context

A rise then a bonus, or a discount then tax, is two changes in turn. Check which amount each percentage applies to.

Worded problems often describe two changes in sequence without using the word "successive" - a wage that rises then a bonus is added as a percentage, or a price that is discounted and then has tax added. The key sign is that the second percentage is described as happening to the new amount, not to the original - read carefully to check which amount each percentage applies to. Once identified, solve exactly as before: build both multipliers, multiply them together, and apply the combined multiplier to the original amount in one step.

Finding an actual final amount, not just the combined percentage

Many questions want the final amount, not the percentage. Apply the combined multiplier to the original amount in one step. e.g. £200×1.06×1.03=£218.36

Some questions ask for the combined percentage change, but many ask for the actual final amount in pounds, kg, or another unit. In that case, apply the combined multiplier directly to the original amount rather than stopping at the percentage - the combined multiplier IS the tool for finding the final amount in one multiplication, so there's no need to work out the two changes separately and add them.
