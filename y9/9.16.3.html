The Or Rule

The and-rule multiplies; the or-rule adds – but only once the overlap between two events has been accounted for. Today is about knowing when to subtract that overlap, and when there is none to subtract.

By the end you’ll know…
  • For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B).
  • Where the events can both happen, the overlap has been counted twice, so P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B).
  • In set notation 'A or B' is the union, A ∪ B, and the overlap is the intersection.
  • 'And' multiplies and 'or' adds, which is the quickest way to tell which rule a question needs.

Mutually exclusive or

For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B). There is no overlap to worry about, since the events cannot both happen.

Overlapping or

When events can both happen, the overlap is counted twice. So P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B).

Where the events can both happen, the overlap has been counted twice, so P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B). "And" multiplies and "or" adds, which is the quickest way to tell which rule a question needs.

When events overlap

Where two events CAN both happen, adding P(A) and P(B) double-counts the overlap. Subtracting P(A and B) once removes exactly that double-count.

Set notation

In set notation, "or" is the union, A ∪ B. The overlap is the intersection. Read for "and" versus "or" to choose the rule.

In set notation "A or B" is the union, A ∪ B, and the overlap is the intersection. Reading a question for "and" versus "or" is the fastest way to choose the right rule.
