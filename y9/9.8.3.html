Difference of Two Squares

x2−9 has no x term, so last lesson's search for a pair seems to have nothing to work with. It still factorises, because it is one square minus another.

By the end you’ll know…
  • x2−a2 factorises as (x+a)(x−a), because expanding it cancels the two middle terms.
  • It is recognised by two square terms separated by a minus sign, with no x term.
  • A common factor is always taken out before factorising any further.
  • Removing a common factor can reveal a difference of two squares that was hidden: 2x2−18 =2(x2−9) =2(x+3)(x−3).

Why the middle terms vanish

Expanding (x+a)(x−a) gives x2 −ax +ax −a2
The two middle terms cancel, leaving x2−a2

Spotting one

A difference of two squares is two square terms with a minus between them. It has no x term.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Common factor first

Always take out a common factor before anything else. What is left may then factorise again.

A hidden difference of two squares

2x2−18 has no square terms until the 2 comes out. Then 2(x2−9) becomes 2(x+3)(x−3).

WORKED EXAMPLE C
Factorise fully 3x2−75
Take out the factor 33x2−75=3(x2−25)
=3(x+5)(x−5)x2−25 is the difference of two squares.
