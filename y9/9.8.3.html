Difference of Two Squares

x² - 9 has no x term, so last lesson's search for a pair seems to have nothing to work with. It still factorises, because it is one square minus another.

By the end you’ll know…
  • x² - a² factorises as (x + a)(x - a), because expanding it cancels the two middle terms.
  • It is recognised by two square terms separated by a minus sign, with no x term.
  • A common factor is always taken out before factorising any further.
  • Removing a common factor can reveal a difference of two squares that was hidden: 2x² - 18 = 2(x² - 9) = 2(x + 3)(x - 3).

02

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Why the middle terms vanish

Expanding (x + a)(x - a) gives x² - ax + ax - a². The two middle terms cancel, leaving x² - a².

Spotting one

A difference of two squares is two square terms with a minus between them. It has no x term.

WORKED EXAMPLE A
Factorise x² - 49.
Find the square rootsx and 7
x² - 49 = (x + 7)(x - 7)
Check by expandingx² - 7x + 7x - 49 = x² - 49
WORKED EXAMPLE B
Factorise 9x² - 25.
Find the square roots3x and 5
9x² - 25 = (3x + 5)(3x - 5)
Check by expanding9x² - 15x + 15x - 25 = 9x² - 25

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Common factor first

Always take out a common factor before anything else. What is left may then factorise again.

A hidden difference of two squares

2x² - 18 has no square terms until the 2 comes out. Then 2(x² - 9) becomes 2(x + 3)(x - 3).

WORKED EXAMPLE C
Factorise fully 3x² - 75.
Take out the factor 33x² - 75 = 3(x² - 25)
= 3(x + 5)(x - 5) x² - 25 is the difference of two squares.
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Factorise fully 2x² + 10x + 12.
Take out the factor 22x² + 10x + 12 = 2(x² + 5x + 6)
