Topic 9.3 · Angles in Polygons · Lesson 4 of 4

Polygons and Parallel Lines

Roof trusses, bridge girders and cross-hatched fencing all combine straight parallel lines with polygon shapes in the same diagram - to find a missing angle in a real structural diagram you need to switch fluently between polygon angle facts and parallel line angle facts, sometimes both within the same figure, and the same idea of matching things correctly (not just by how they look on the page) carries over to similar shapes and their scale factors.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Where a polygon's sides meet parallel lines, the alternate and corresponding angle rules apply as usual.
  • A polygon's own angle facts and the parallel line facts combine within a single diagram.
  • Similar shapes have equal corresponding angles and corresponding sides in the same ratio.
  • The scale factor is the ratio of a length on one shape to the matching length on the other, and multiplying by it gives a missing length.

Combining the two angle worlds

Parallel-line rules still apply when a polygon is present. Use them only on angles the parallel lines form. Use polygon facts only on the polygon's own angles.

When a polygon's side lines are extended and cross a pair of parallel lines, the alternate and corresponding angle rules still apply exactly as they always have - a parallel-line diagram does not change those rules just because a polygon is also present. Use the parallel line facts ONLY on angles that are actually formed by the parallel lines (and the line crossing them); use the polygon's own angle facts (exterior sum 360, interior sum (n-2)×180) on the polygon's own angles.

Watch for hidden triangles

Extended sides and a parallel line can form a new triangle. Its angles sum to 180°, not the polygon's total.

Sometimes a polygon diagram combined with parallel lines creates a NEW triangle where none of the polygon's sides directly form it - for example, where two extended sides of a polygon meet a parallel line. That triangle's angles sum to 180° like any triangle - it is NOT part of the polygon's own interior angle sum, so do not apply the polygon's (n-2)×180 formula to it.
WORKED EXAMPLE A
A regular hexagon has one side extended so that it crosses a pair of parallel lines. Between the two lines, the extended side makes an angle of 70° with the first parallel line. Find the angle alternate to it at the second parallel line. The angle is formed by the parallel lines and the extended side, so the parallel-line fact applies - the hexagon does not change it.
Alternate angles are equalangle = 70°

Similar shapes

Similar shapes have equal angles and sides in the same ratio. Match sides by the angles opposite them, not by position.

Two shapes are similar if their corresponding angles are equal AND their corresponding sides are all in the same ratio - one shape is an enlargement (or reduction) of the other, same shape, different size. Corresponding sides are the ones that sit opposite equal corresponding angles, not just the ones that happen to be drawn in the same position on the page.

Scale factor

Scale factor = new length ÷ original length, for a matching pair. Multiply any original length by it to find its match.

The scale factor is the ratio of a length on one shape to the MATCHING (corresponding) length on the other: scale factor = new length / original length, using a matching pair of sides. Once you know the scale factor, MULTIPLY any length on the original shape by it to find the matching length on the enlarged or reduced shape.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Triangles A and B are similar. The 5 cm side on A matches 15 cm on B. Find the side on B that matches 8 cm.
