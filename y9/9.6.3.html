Topic 9.6 · Right-Angled Trigonometry · Lesson 3 of 5

Finding the Hypotenuse

Yesterday the unknown was always on top of the ratio. Today it moves to the bottom - which changes the algebra from a one-step multiplication to a divide, and means you have to check the diagram carefully before you can even start.

By the end you’ll know…
  • With the unknown on the denominator, the ratio takes two steps: multiply both sides by the unknown, then divide.
  • sin(x)=opphyp with the opposite known therefore gives hyp=opp÷sin(x).
  • The unknown sits on the denominator when finding the hypotenuse with sin or cos, or the adjacent with tan.
  • Which case applies is decided from the labelled diagram before any calculation starts.
  • The hypotenuse must come out larger than either other side, which checks the answer.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Why the unknown-on-the-bottom case takes two algebra steps

An unknown on the bottom takes two steps instead of one. Multiply both sides by it, then divide by the ratio, giving hyp=oppsin(x).

In sin(x)=opphyp, if the hypotenuse is unknown, it is sitting on the denominator of the fraction, not the numerator. To isolate it: first multiply both sides by hyp, giving hyp×sin(x)=opp; then divide both sides by sin(x), giving hyp=oppsin(x). The two steps are always the same for this case - multiply by the unknown, then divide by the ratio - so the working can be written straight to hyp=oppsin(x) once you recognise the pattern. The same applies to cos: hyp=adjcos(x).

Checking the answer makes sense

The hypotenuse is always the longest side. So hyp=oppsin(x) must come out longer than the known side.

The hypotenuse is always the longest side of a right-angled triangle, because it is opposite the largest angle (the right angle). So once hyp=oppsin(x) has been calculated, the answer must come out larger than the known side used to find it. If it doesn't, an operation has gone wrong somewhere - most often a multiply where a divide was needed, or the wrong ratio chosen.

Learn

Deciding numerator or denominator before you start

First decide where the unknown sits in the ratio. On the top, multiply. On the bottom, divide the known side by the ratio.

Given a right-angled triangle with one side and one angle known, and a second side to find, the first decision is not which ratio to use but where the unknown sits. Write the ratio in full with the unknown's letter in place: if it appears on the top, one step (multiply) solves it; if it appears on the bottom, two steps (multiply then divide, which simplifies to a single divide of the known side by the ratio) solve it. This decision comes from the diagram, before any arithmetic.

The same triangle can ask for different things

The same side and angle can find either other side. Check where the unknown sits every time, however familiar the triangle.

The same pair of a known side and a known angle can be used to find either of the other two sides - which one depends only on which side the question asks for, not on the triangle itself. Some questions ask for a leg (numerator case), others for the hypotenuse (denominator case), so the identification step in Slide 1 has to be done freshly every time, even when the triangle looks familiar.
