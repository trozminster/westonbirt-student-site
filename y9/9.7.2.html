Topic 9.7 · Expanding Brackets · Lesson 2 of 6

Special Cases and Contexts

Some double brackets, like (x+a)(x−a) or (x+a)2, come up so often that it pays to recognise the pattern rather than expand term by term every time - especially once those patterns start appearing as the area or perimeter of a shape whose sides are given algebraically.

By the end you’ll know…
  • (x+a)(x−a) gives x2−a2, the difference of two squares, because the two middle terms cancel.
  • (x+a)2 gives x2 +2ax +a2, and (x−a)2 gives x2−2ax+a2
  • Recognising these forms saves expanding them term by term every time.
  • A perimeter adds all the side lengths and an area multiplies two of them, so both give expressions to expand and simplify.

Difference of two squares

In (x+a)(x−a) the middle terms −ax and ax cancel. So the answer is x2−a2, the difference of two squares.

(x+a)(x−a) is a special case, and expanding it fully shows why - Every term times every term: x times x=x2, x times −a=−ax, a times x=ax and a times −a=−a2
The two middle terms, −ax and ax, are opposites and cancel to zero, leaving x2−a2 - the difference of two squares. Recognising this pattern (two brackets with identical terms, one addition and one subtraction) means the answer can be written straight down as (first term)² minus (second term)², without expanding term by term.

Squared brackets, both signs

(x−a)2 =x2 −2ax +a2: the last term stays positive. One bracket squared keeps its middle term. Two different brackets, (x+a)(x−a), lose it.

(x+a)2 expands to x2 +2ax +a2, with the middle term added. (x−a)2 expands to x2 −2ax +a2, with the middle term subtracted, but the last term a2 is still positive because a negative times a negative is positive. It's easy to muddle (x−a)2 with the difference of two squares because both involve subtraction - the test is whether there are two different brackets multiplied together (difference of two squares) or one bracket squared, which keeps its middle term.

Area and perimeter as expressions

Perimeter adds the side lengths. Area multiplies two of them, often as a double bracket.

When a shape's sides are given as algebraic expressions, its perimeter is found by adding all the side lengths, and its area by multiplying two of them (for a rectangle, width times length). Both of these give an expression that may need expanding and simplifying - a perimeter usually just needs the brackets already present to be expanded, while an area often means multiplying two brackets together as a double bracket.

Choosing the right operation

The usual error is forming the wrong expression, not the algebra. Check whether the question asks for perimeter or area first. An x2 in a perimeter means the wrong operation.

The most common error in these contexts isn't the algebra itself but forming the wrong expression in the first place - adding side lengths when the question asks for area, or multiplying when it asks for perimeter. Always check which is being asked for before starting to expand: perimeter has units like cm (a length), area has units like cm² (a length squared) - if a working expression for a 'perimeter' ends up with an x2 term, that's a sign the wrong operation was used.
