Topic 9.17 · Circle Theorems · Lesson 5 of 6

Alternate Segment

One more theorem, plus the skill that ties this whole topic together: deciding which theorem a diagram actually calls for, since a full problem may need more than one in sequence.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A chord drawn from the point where a tangent touches divides the circle into two segments.
  • The angle between the tangent and that chord equals the angle in the alternate segment, the one on the other side of the chord.
  • The alternate segment is identified from the chord, not from how the diagram happens to look.
  • Which theorem applies is decided by what the diagram contains: a diameter, the centre, a cyclic quadrilateral, a tangent, or two angles on one arc.

The alternate segment theorem

The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment. That segment is on the other side of the chord.

When a chord is drawn from the point where a tangent touches a circle, the angle between the tangent and the chord equals the angle in the ALTERNATE segment – the segment on the OTHER side of the chord from the angle.

Identifying the alternate segment

Find the alternate segment from the chord, not the look of the diagram. The equal angle is across the chord from the tangent angle.

The segment is identified from the chord, not from how the diagram happens to look. Trace the chord, find the angle in the segment on the far side of it from the tangent angle – that is the equal angle.
TANGENT-CHORD ANGLE
A tangent touches a circle at T. A chord TA is drawn, and the tangent-chord angle is 48°. B is a point on the circumference in the alternate segment. Find angle TBA.

Choosing the right theorem

What the diagram contains decides the theorem. Look for a diameter, the centre, a cyclic quadrilateral or a tangent.

Which theorem applies is decided by what the diagram actually CONTAINS – a diameter (semicircle), the centre marked with two angles on the same arc (angle at centre or same segment), all four vertices of a quadrilateral on the circle (cyclic quadrilateral), a tangent (tangent-radius or alternate segment), or two angles standing on one arc.

A quick checklist

Ask in order: a diameter, the centre, a tangent, a quadrilateral? The answers narrow down the theorem before any calculation.

Is there a diameter? A centre point with radii? A tangent line? A full quadrilateral inscribed? Answering these in order narrows down which theorem (or theorems) the question is actually testing, before any calculation starts.
TANGENT AND DIAMETER
A circle has centre O. AB is a diameter. A tangent touches the circle at C, and chord CB is drawn. The tangent-chord angle at C is 40°. Find angle CAB.
Alternate segment theoremangle CAB=40°Angle CAB stands on chord CB, on the opposite side from the tangent angle.
Check with the semicircleangle ACB=90°
angle ABC=180−90−40=50°
YOUR TURN · e
DEFG is a cyclic quadrilateral with angle F=100°. The tangent at D meets chord DE at 58°. Which theorem finds angle DGE? Find it.
Alternate segment theorem: angle DGE=58° (the cyclic quadrilateral fact about angle F is not needed for this particular angle)
YOUR TURN · f
HJ is a diameter and K is on the circle. Which theorem finds angle HKJ? Find it.
Angle in a semicircle: angle HKJ=90°, since HJ is a diameter and K is on the circumference
YOUR TURN · g
A, B, C and D lie on a circle. The tangent at A meets chord AB at 58°, with C in the alternate segment and D on the minor arc AB. Find angle ACB, then angle ADB.
Angle ACB=58° (alternate segment); angle ADB =180−58 =122° (opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral ACBD)
YOUR TURN · h
AB is a diameter and C is on the circle. A tangent touches the circle at C, and the tangent-chord angle with chord CB is 34°. Find angle CAB, then angle ABC.
Angle CAB=34° (alternate segment); angle ACB=90° (angle in a semicircle), so angle ABC =180−90−34 =56°
