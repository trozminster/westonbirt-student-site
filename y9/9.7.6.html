Algebraic Fraction Operations

Multiplying and dividing algebraic fractions follows exactly the same rules as multiplying and dividing numerical ones - multiply straight across, or flip and multiply for division - but the real payoff comes from factorising first, so common factors can be cancelled before the multiplication ever has to be done.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Algebraic fractions multiply by multiplying numerators and denominators, exactly as numerical ones do.
  • Dividing means multiplying by the reciprocal.
  • Cancelling common factors before multiplying keeps the expressions manageable.
  • Where a numerator or denominator is a sum, it is factorised first so the common factors become visible.

Multiplying algebraic fractions

Multiply algebraic fractions like number fractions: tops together, bottoms together. Cancel common factors first where you can.

Two algebraic fractions multiply exactly as numerical fractions do - the numerators make the new numerator and the denominators make the new denominator, e.g. 23×45=815
So x3×5x =5x3x. Where possible, cancel common factors before multiplying, not just after, to keep the numbers and expressions smaller and more manageable.

Dividing algebraic fractions

To divide, multiply by the reciprocal of the second fraction. So x4÷x8 =x4×8x =2

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal (the fraction flipped upside down): ab divided by cd is ab×dc. So x4÷x8 becomes x4×8x =8x4x, which cancels (common factors of 4 and x) to 2
This 'flip and multiply' rule applies whether the fractions contain numbers, letters, or both.

Factorising before multiplying

Factorise any sum before multiplying. This shows common factors that can cancel across the fractions.

When a numerator or denominator in a multiplication or division of algebraic fractions is a sum, like x+3, it must be factorised first, exactly as with a single fraction - the common factors that let things cancel are hidden inside the sum until it's rewritten as a product. For x2+3x5×10x+3, the first numerator factorises to x(x+3), which then shares a factor of (x+3) with the second denominator, letting it cancel before any multiplying happens.

Cancelling across the whole calculation

Cancel across the whole calculation, any top against any bottom. For division, flip the second fraction before you look.

Once every numerator and denominator involved is factorised, look across the WHOLE calculation - not just within one fraction - for factors that appear on a top and a bottom anywhere, and cancel them before multiplying what's left. This keeps the numbers and brackets manageable and avoids expanding a large, unfactorisable mess. For division, remember to take the reciprocal of the second fraction first, so its numerator and denominator swap places, before starting to look for what factorises and cancels.
