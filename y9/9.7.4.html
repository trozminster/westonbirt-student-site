Topic 9.7 · Expanding Brackets · Lesson 4 of 6

Factorising Fully and Common Brackets

Expanding turns a bracket into separate terms; factorising undoes that, rewriting an expression as a product with a bracket back in it - and it's only useful if the bracket taken out is the biggest one available, or the whole thing was pointless.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Factorising is the inverse of expanding: the expression is rewritten as a product with a bracket.
  • The factor taken out is the highest common factor of every term, letters as well as numbers.
  • Fully factorised means the terms left inside share no common factor, so the HCF of 6x² and 9x is 3x.
  • A bracket in a factorised expression is itself a factor of the whole expression.
  • Expanding the result checks the factorisation.

Factorising as the inverse of expanding

Factorising is the inverse of expanding. Take the HCF of every term outside the bracket. Expand back out to check.

Factorising rewrites an expression as a product, usually a number andor letter multiplied by a bracket, such that expanding the result gives back the original expression. To factorise, find the highest common factor (HCF) of every term - both the numbers and any letters - and write it outside a bracket containing what's left when each term is divided by that HCF. Checking by expanding the result back out catches most sign or coefficient errors before they're marked wrong.

Fully factorised, including powers of a letter

Fully factorised means nothing common is left inside the bracket. 10x²+4x = 2x(5x+2), not 2(5x²+2x). A letter's HCF takes the lowest power present.

'Fully factorised' means the terms left inside the bracket share no common factor at all - not just numerically, but in any letters too. For 10x²+4x, the numerical HCF of 10 and 4 is 2, but both terms also share a factor of x (since x² = x × x), so the full HCF is 2x, giving 2x(5x+2) - stopping at just 2(5x²+2x) is a common factor, but not the highest one. When the same letter appears with different powers, like 9x³ and 6x², the letter part of the HCF is the LOWER power that appears in every term (here, x²), since that's the most that can be divided out of both.
TAKING OUT THE HCF
Factorise 6x²+9x fully.
Find the HCF3x Numbers: the factors of 6 are 1, 2, 3, 6 and of 9 are 1, 3, 9, so the HCF is 3. Letters: both terms contain at least one x (x² = x times x), so the letter part is x.
Divide each term by 3x2x and 3 6x² ÷ 3x = 2x
9x ÷ 3x = 3.
Take out 3x6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)
Check by expanding3x(2x + 3) = 6x² + 9x Matches the original.
HIGHER POWERS
Now factorise 8x³+12x² fully.
Find the HCF4x² Numbers: the factors of 8 are 1, 2, 4, 8 and of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12, so the HCF is 4. Letters: the terms contain x³ and x², and the lower power present in both is x².
Divide each term by 4x^22x and 3 8x³ ÷ 4x² = 2x
12x² ÷ 4x² = 3.
Take out 4x^28x³ + 12x² = 4x²(2x + 3)
Check by expanding4x²(2x + 3) = 8x³ + 12x² Matches the original.

A bracket as a factor

In 3x(2x+3), the bracket (2x+3) is a factor too. A shared bracket can be factored out like a number or letter.

Once an expression is factorised, like 3x(2x+3), the bracket (2x+3) is itself a factor of the whole expression, in exactly the same way that 3x is - the expression is a product of the two. This matters when simplifying: if the same bracket appears as a factor in two parts of a larger expression (for instance, both terms of a sum each have a factor of (2x+3)), that shared bracket can be factored out just like a shared number or letter would be.

Using factorised brackets to simplify

A bracket shared by every term is a common factor. So 5x(x+2) + 3(x+2) = (x+2)(5x+3).

Spotting a bracket as a common factor lets a more complicated expression be simplified without expanding everything first. For example, in 5x(x+2) + 3(x+2), both terms share the factor (x+2): treat (x+2) as if it were a single letter being factored out, giving (x+2)(5x+3) - the same HCF idea as before, but with a bracket playing the role of the common factor instead of a number or a single letter.
COMMON BRACKET FACTOR
Simplify 4x(x+5) + 3(x+5) by identifying and factoring out the common bracket.
Spot the common bracket(x + 5) Both terms, 4x(x + 5) and 3(x + 5), contain the factor (x + 5). Treat it exactly like a common number or letter.
Take out (x + 5)4x(x + 5) + 3(x + 5) = (x + 5)(4x + 3)
Check by expanding(x + 5)(4x + 3) = 4x² + 3x + 20x + 15
Collect like terms= 4x² + 23x + 15 Expanding the original also gives 4x² + 20x + 3x + 15 = 4x² + 23x + 15. Matches.
A SUBTRACTION
Now simplify 7(2x-1) - x(2x-1).
Spot the common bracket(2x - 1)
Take out (2x - 1)7(2x - 1) - x(2x - 1) = (2x - 1)(7 - x) The order inside the second bracket follows the order the terms appeared: 7 first, then -x.
Check by expanding(2x - 1)(7 - x) = 14x - 2x² - 7 + x
Collect like terms= -2x² + 15x - 7 Expanding the original: 14x - 7 - 2x² + x = -2x² + 15x - 7. Matches.
