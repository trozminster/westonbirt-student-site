Topic 9.7 · Expanding Brackets · Lesson 4 of 6

Factorising Fully and Common Brackets

Expanding turns a bracket into separate terms; factorising undoes that, rewriting an expression as a product with a bracket back in it - and it's only useful if the bracket taken out is the biggest one available, or the whole thing was pointless.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Factorising is the inverse of expanding: the expression is rewritten as a product with a bracket.
  • The factor taken out is the highest common factor of every term, letters as well as numbers.
  • Fully factorised means the terms left inside share no common factor, so the HCF of 6x2 and 9x is 3x.
  • A bracket in a factorised expression is itself a factor of the whole expression.
  • Expanding the result checks the factorisation.

Factorising as the inverse of expanding

Factorising is the inverse of expanding. Take the HCF of every term outside the bracket. Expand back out to check.

Factorising rewrites an expression as a product, usually a number and/or letter multiplied by a bracket, such that expanding the result gives back the original expression. To factorise, find the highest common factor (HCF) of every term - both the numbers and any letters - and write it outside a bracket containing what's left when each term is divided by that HCF. Checking by expanding the result back out catches most sign or coefficient errors before they're marked wrong.

Fully factorised, including powers of a letter

Fully factorised means nothing common is left inside the bracket. 10x2+4x =2x(5x+2), not 2(5x2+2x). A letter's HCF takes the lowest power present.

'Fully factorised' means the terms left inside the bracket share no common factor at all - not just numerically, but in any letters too. For 10x2+4x, the numerical HCF of 10 and 4 is 2, but both terms also share a factor of x (since x2=x×x), so the full HCF is 2x, giving 2x(5x+2) - stopping at just 2(5x2+2x) is a common factor, but not the highest one. When the same letter appears with different powers, like 9x3 and 6x2, the letter part of the HCF is the LOWER power that appears in every term (here, x2), since that's the most that can be divided out of both.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

A bracket as a factor

In 3x(2x+3), the bracket (2x+3) is a factor too. A shared bracket can be factored out like a number or letter.

Once an expression is factorised, like 3x(2x+3), the bracket (2x+3) is itself a factor of the whole expression, in exactly the same way that 3x is - the expression is a product of the two. This matters when simplifying: if the same bracket appears as a factor in two parts of a larger expression (for instance, both terms of a sum each have a factor of (2x+3)), that shared bracket can be factored out just like a shared number or letter would be.

Using factorised brackets to simplify

A bracket shared by every term is a common factor. So 5x(x+2) +3(x+2) =(x+2)(5x+3).

Spotting a bracket as a common factor lets a more complicated expression be simplified without expanding everything first. For example, in 5x(x+2) +3(x+2), both terms share the factor (x+2): treat (x+2) as if it were a single letter being factored out, giving (x+2)(5x+3) - the same HCF idea as before, but with a bracket playing the role of the common factor instead of a number or a single letter.
