Mixed Percentage Problems

This lesson doesn't teach a new percentage skill - it brings every method from the unit together. The hardest part of a mixed problem is rarely the calculation itself; it's deciding which of the several methods you now know actually applies. You'll practise diagnosing what's unknown before calculating, and switching fluently between fractions, decimals, percentages and ratios, since real problems rarely announce which representation is easiest.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Percentage problems differ by what is unknown: the change, the original amount, or the final amount.
  • An unknown final amount multiplies by the multiplier; an unknown original divides by it.
  • An unknown percentage comes from comparing the change with the original.
  • Fractions, decimals, percentages and ratios all express the same multiplicative relationships, so a problem can be converted into whichever form is easiest.

Diagnosing what's unknown before choosing a method

First decide which is unknown: the change, the original or the final amount. That choice decides the method.

Every percentage problem in this unit has three possible unknowns: the CHANGE (the increase or decrease itself), the ORIGINAL amount (before the change), or the FINAL amount (after the change). Which one is missing determines the method: if the final amount is unknown, multiply the original by the multiplier; if the original amount is unknown, divide the final amount by the multiplier (reverse percentage); if the percentage change itself is unknown, find (change / original) × 100. Reading a problem to identify which of the three is missing - before writing any calculation - is the single most useful habit in this unit.

The same multiplier serves multiple methods

The multiplier is built the same way for every method. Only the operation changes: multiply, divide, or compare two amounts.

Whichever unknown you're solving for, the multiplier itself is built the same way as always (1 + p100 for an increase, 1 - p100 for a decrease). What changes between methods isn't the multiplier - it's the operation applied to it (multiply or divide) or whether you're comparing two known amounts to find a missing percentage. Before calculating, it can help to name out loud which of the three quantities (change, original, final) the question gives you and which it wants.
IDENTIFY THE UNKNOWN
A problem states: 'A price rises from 80 pounds to 92 pounds. Find the percentage increase.' Identify what's unknown and solve.
I am given the original and the final, and want the percentage change. Since the percentage is unknown, use (change / original) × 100.
Find the change92 - 80 = 12
Use the percentage change rule% increase = 1280 × 100
= 0.15 × 100
Percentage increase= 15%
YOUR TURN · a
A price rises from £50 to £60. Find the percentage increase.
given the original and the final, the percentage is unknown: change = 10, and (10 ÷ 50) × 100 = 20% increase.
YOUR TURN · b
A price falls from £120 to £102. Find the percentage decrease.
given the original (120) and the final (102), the percentage is unknown: change = 18, and (18 ÷ 120) × 100 = 15% decrease.
YOUR TURN · c
After a 25% decrease, a price is £45. Find the original price. Notice the unknown is now the ORIGINAL, not the percentage: divide by a multiplier.
given: the new price £45 after a 25% decrease; unknown: the original. Multiplier = 0.75; 45 ÷ 0.75 = 60, so the original price was £60.

Learn

Fractions, decimals, percentages and ratios all express the same relationship

75%, 0.75, 34 and the ratio 3:4 (part to whole) all mean three quarters. Convert to whichever form makes the arithmetic simplest.

A percentage, a decimal multiplier, an equivalent fraction, and a ratio can all describe the exact same multiplicative relationship - 75%, 0.75, 34, and the ratio 3:4 (part to whole) all say "three-quarters of the amount". A mixed problem can often be solved more easily by converting into whichever of these four forms makes the arithmetic simplest, rather than sticking rigidly to percentages throughout.

Choosing the easiest representation for a given problem

Some percentages are easy fractions: 25% = 14. Successive and reverse changes are easier with decimal multipliers. Check every conversion is exact.

Some percentages convert to very manageable fractions (50% = 12, 25% = 14, 10% = 110), which can make mental calculation faster than using a decimal multiplier; other problems, especially those involving successive changes or reverse percentages, are far easier to manage with decimal multipliers, since fractions don't combine by simple multiplication as tidily when the denominators differ. Convert carefully and check the conversion is exact - a common source of error is converting a fraction to a decimal or percentage inaccurately, which then carries an error through the rest of the problem.
SHARING IN A RATIO
A recipe uses flour and sugar in the ratio 3:2. The total mixture weighs 500 g. Find the weight of sugar. Use the ratio, not a percentage, since the ratio is the easiest form here.
Add the parts3 + 2 = 5
Share 500 g into 5one part = 500 ÷ 5
= 100 g
Take 2 partssugar = 2 × 100
Weight of sugar= 200 g
Check with a percentage40% of 500 = 200 g Sugar should be 2 ÷ 5 = 40% of the total. Matches.
YOUR TURN · d
A fruit drink mixes juice and water in the ratio 2:3. The total is 400 ml. Find the volume of juice, using whichever representation (ratio, fraction, or percentage) is easiest, and check your answer using a different representation from the one you used to solve it.
juice is 2 of 5 parts: 400 ÷ 5 = 80 ml per part, so 2 × 80 = 160 ml of juice. Check with a percentage: 2 ÷ 5 = 40%, and 40% of 400 = 160 ml.
YOUR TURN · e
A class has 25 students. 35 of the class are girls. Of the girls, 40% wear glasses. Find how many girls wear glasses. Notice the 40% is of the girls, not of all 25.
girls = 35 of 25 = 15; 40% of 15 = 0.4 × 15 = 6, so 6 girls wear glasses.
