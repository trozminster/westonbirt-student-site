Topic 9.17 · Circle Theorems · Lesson 3 of 6

Cyclic Quadrilaterals and Chords

A cyclic quadrilateral brings a new angle fact, and a chord brings a new way to combine circle theorems with Pythagoras. Both rely on facts you already know, applied inside a circle.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A cyclic quadrilateral has all four of its vertices on the circumference.
  • Its opposite angles sum to 180 degrees.
  • A chord is a line joining two points on the circumference.
  • The perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre, so a radius drawn perpendicular to a chord bisects it.
  • That creates a right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras finds a missing length.

Cyclic quadrilaterals

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on one circle. Its opposite angles sum to 180°. This is a new theorem, one pair at a time.

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on the circumference of the same circle. Its opposite angles sum to 180° – this is a new theorem, not the ordinary "angles in a quadrilateral sum to 360," and it applies to ONE PAIR of opposite angles at a time.

Chords and the perpendicular bisector

A chord joins two points on the circumference. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord.

A chord joins two points on the circumference. The perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre, so a radius drawn perpendicular to a chord bisects it exactly in half, creating a right-angled triangle Pythagoras can be used on.

The chord bisector triangle

The perpendicular from the centre makes two congruent right-angled triangles. Each has the radius as its hypotenuse.

Drawing the radius from the centre perpendicular to a chord creates two congruent right-angled triangles, each with the radius as hypotenuse, half the chord as one side, and the perpendicular distance from the centre as the other.

Solving with Pythagoras

Pythagoras then finds the missing length. It could be the radius, half the chord, or the distance from the centre.

Once that right-angled triangle is identified, Pythagoras finds whichever length is missing – the radius, half the chord, or the distance from the centre – exactly as with any right-angled triangle.
CHORD LENGTH
A circle has radius 13 cm. A chord AB is 10 cm from the centre O (measured perpendicular to the chord). Find the length of the chord.
Use Pythagoras in triangle OMAAM = √(13² - 10²) The perpendicular from O meets the chord at M, making a right-angled triangle: the radius 13 is the hypotenuse, the distance 10 is one side.
= √(169 - 100)
= √69
The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chordAB = 2√69
Chord length to 3 s.f.AB = 16.6 cm
YOUR TURN · e
A circle has radius 17 cm. A chord CD is 8 cm from the centre. Find the length of the chord.
Half chord = √(17² - 8²) = √(289-64) = √(225) = 15; full chord = 30 cm
YOUR TURN · f
A circle has radius 25 cm. A chord EF has length 48 cm. Find the perpendicular distance from the centre to the chord.
Half chord = 24; distance = √(25² - 24²) = √(625-576) = √(49) = 7 cm
YOUR TURN · g
A chord of length 24 cm is 5 cm from the centre of a circle. Find the radius.
Half chord = 12; radius = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 cm
YOUR TURN · h
A circle has radius 25 cm. Parallel chords of 48 cm and 14 cm lie on opposite sides of the centre. Find the distance between them.
√(25² - 24²) = 7 and √(25² - 7²) = 24; distance = 7 + 24 = 31 cm

Why does a chord bisector problem always produce a right-angled triangle?
The radius is drawn perpendicular to the chord, and a perpendicular from the centre to a chord always bisects it, so the radius, half the chord and the centre-to-chord distance form a right angle at the midpoint.
