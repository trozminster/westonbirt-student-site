The Sine Rule for Angles

A surveyor who measures two sides and one angle needs the other angles of the triangle. Today we turn the sine rule round to find angles, and meet the case where two triangles fit the same facts.

By the end you’ll know…
  • To find an angle, the sine rule is written with the sines on top: sin A÷a = sin B÷b.
  • This gives sin A, and then A = sin⁻¹ of that value.
  • Because sin x = sin (180−x), a value of sin A fits two angles, A and 180−A.
  • The obtuse angle is possible only if it leaves room for the other angles, so when the given angle is opposite the shorter given side there can be two triangles: the ambiguous case.
By the end you can
G7Use the sine rule to find a missing angle in a triangle that is not right-angled.
G8Decide whether the ambiguous case gives one or two possible triangles, and find both angles when two exist.

Sines on top to find an angle

To find an angle, write the rule with sines on top. Then one step leaves sin A alone.

Undo the sine at the end

The rule gives the value of sin A, not A. The inverse sine turns that value into the angle.

WORKED EXAMPLE A

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

One sine, two angles

An angle and 180° minus it have the same sine. So a value of sin C can give an acute and an obtuse angle.

Does the obtuse angle fit

The obtuse angle counts only if the triangle's angles still add to 180°. That happens when the given angle faces the shorter given side.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
In triangle ABC, B=40°, b=7 cm and c=10 cm. Find both possible values of C.
Sine rulesin C=10 sin 40°÷7=0.918

Obtuse angle180°−66.7°=113.3°

C=66.7° or 113.3°
