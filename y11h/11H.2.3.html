The Sine Rule for Angles

A surveyor who measures two sides and one angle needs the other angles of the triangle. Today we turn the sine rule round to find angles, and meet the case where two triangles fit the same facts.

By the end you’ll know…
  • To find an angle, the sine rule is written with the sines on top: sinA÷a =sinB÷b.
  • This gives sinA, and then A = sin⁻¹ of that value.
  • Because sinx =sin(180−x), a value of sinA fits two angles, A and 180−A.
  • The obtuse angle is possible only if it leaves room for the other angles, so when the given angle is opposite the shorter given side there can be two triangles: the ambiguous case.

Sines on top to find an angle

To find an angle, write the rule with sines on top. Then one step leaves sinA alone.

Undo the sine at the end

The rule gives the value of sinA, not A. The inverse sine turns that value into the angle.

WORKED EXAMPLE A
In triangle ABC, a=7 cm, b=9 cm and B=50°. Find A.
Sines on topsinA7=sin50°9
Multiply by 7sinA=7sin50°÷9≈0.596
A=36.6°

Sines on topsinP12=sin70°15
Multiply by 12sinP=12sin70°÷15≈0.752
P=48.7°

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

One sine, two angles

An angle and 180° minus it have the same sine. So a value of sinC can give an acute and an obtuse angle.

Does the obtuse angle fit

The obtuse angle counts only if the triangle's angles still add to 180°. That happens when the given angle faces the shorter given side.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
In triangle ABC, B=40°, b=7 cm and c=10 cm. Find both possible values of C.
Sine rulesinC=10sin40°÷7≈0.918
Acute angleC=66.7°
Obtuse angle180°−66.7°=113.3°
Check the obtuse angle40°+113.3°<180°
C=66.7° or 113.3°
WORKED EXAMPLE D

Sine rulesinC=10sin40°÷12≈0.536
Acute angleC=32.4°

One triangle onlyC=32.4°
