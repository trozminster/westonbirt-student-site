Differentiating after Rewriting

The rule only works on single powers of x, but real expressions arrive as brackets and fractions. Today: rewriting any expression into powers of x first, including negative powers, so the rule always applies.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The rule differentiates one power of x at a time, so an expression is rewritten as a sum of powers of x first.
  • Brackets are expanded before differentiating, never differentiated factor by factor.
  • A fraction with a single term underneath splits into separate terms: x³ + 2xx = x² + 2.
  • A power of x underneath becomes a negative power: 1x = x⁻¹ and 4x² = 4x⁻².
  • The same rule works for negative integer powers: the power multiplies and drops by one, so x⁻² gives -2x⁻³.

Expand before differentiating

The rule works on single powers of x. So brackets are expanded first.

The rule works on one power of x at a time, so a product of brackets is expanded first. (x + 3)(2x - 1) becomes 2x² + 5x - 3, which then differentiates to 4x + 5.

Split a fraction first

A fraction over a single term splits into separate terms. So x³ + 2xx becomes x² + 2.

A fraction with a single term underneath splits into separate terms. So x³ + 2xx becomes x² + 2 before differentiating.

Learn

A power underneath is a negative power

A power underneath becomes a negative power. So 4x² is rewritten as 4x⁻² first.

A power of x in the denominator is written as a negative power before differentiating. So 4x² becomes 4x⁻², which the same rule then handles.

The power still drops by one

The power still comes down and drops by one. So x⁻² gives −2x⁻³, which is −2x³.

The power still multiplies and drops by one, so -2 becomes -3. So x⁻² differentiates to -2x⁻³, which is -2x³.
