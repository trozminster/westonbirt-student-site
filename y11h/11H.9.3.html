Range on a Limited Domain

Many functions only make sense for some inputs, such as a phone's charge over the first 10 hours. Today we find the outputs a limited set of inputs produces, and why a graph's turning point matters.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A function can be defined on a limited domain, such as f(x)=2x+1 for 0≤x≤5, and its range then comes only from those inputs.
  • A linear function's range runs between its outputs at the two ends of the domain: here 1≤f(x)≤11
  • A decreasing function has its least output at the right-hand end, and the range is still written smallest first.
  • A quadratic's least or greatest output is at its turning point when that lies inside the domain, so its range is not found from the ends alone.
  • A strict end of the domain, <, gives a strict end of the range.

Fewer inputs, fewer outputs

A limited domain allows only some inputs. The range then comes only from those inputs.

Smallest first, always

A decreasing line is highest at its left end. The range is still written smallest first.

WORKED EXAMPLE A

Find the range.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The turning point can be inside

A quadratic's lowest output is at its turning point. When that lies in the domain, it bounds the range.

Three outputs to compare

The greatest output is at an end of the domain. So compare both ends with the turning point.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
f(x) =x2−6x+10 for 0≤x≤5
Find the range.
Find the turning pointx=3
f(3) =1dydx =2x−6 =0 gives x=3
halfway between equal outputs works too.
Check both endsf(0)=10
f(5)=5
Range1≤f(x)≤10
WORKED EXAMPLE D
g(x) =x2+4x for 1≤x≤3
Find the range.
Turning point, outside the domainx =−2dydx =2x+4 =0 gives x=−2, which is not in 1≤x≤3, so only the ends matter.
Check both endsg(1)=5
g(3)=21
Range5≤g(x)≤21
