Verifying and Counter-Examples

A pattern can hold a thousand times and still break on the next case, which is why mathematics demands proof. Today: why examples never prove a rule, and how a single example destroys a false one.

By the end you’ll know…
  • To verify a statement is to check that it holds for one particular case.
  • Verifying is not proving: a statement can hold for many cases and still be false.
  • A counter-example is a single case meeting the conditions but not the conclusion, and it disproves the statement.
  • Proving a statement always true needs an argument covering every case, usually an algebraic one.
  • Counter-examples usually sit at the edges: 0, 1, 2, negatives and fractions.

Checking is not proving

Checking values only shows that those values work. The value of n2+n+41 is prime until n=40

Checking a statement for a few values only shows that it holds for those values. The rule n2+n+41 gives a prime for every n from 0 to 39, yet n=40 gives 41×41

One counter-example is enough

A counter-example meets the conditions but breaks the conclusion. One is enough to disprove a statement.

A counter-example meets the statement's conditions but breaks its conclusion. One such case shows the statement is false, however many cases agree with it.

Counter-examples hide at the edges

Algebraic statements often hold for whole numbers and fail at the edges. Zero, one, negatives and fractions are worth trying.

Algebraic statements often hold for most whole numbers and fail at the edges. 0, 1, negatives and fractions between 0 and 1 are where counter-examples hide.

Work out both sides

A false rule can look like a familiar law. Both sides are worked out separately, then compared.

A false rule can look like a familiar law. Working out both sides for one pair of values, such as a=1 and b=2, shows whether they agree.
