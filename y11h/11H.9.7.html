Harder Inverse Functions

Some rules hide x in two places, so no single run backwards through the steps will undo them. Today's inverses use the subject-twice method, and inverses turn into a quick way to solve equations.

By the end you’ll know…
  • When x appears twice in y=f(x), as in y =2x+1x−3, the denominator is cleared, the x terms are collected on one side and x is factorised out, as in changing the subject.
  • The domain of f−1 is the range of f, and the range of f−1 is the domain of f.
  • A function has an inverse only when each output comes from one input, so a quadratic needs a limited domain, such as x≥0, first.
  • An equation such as f−1(x)=4 is solved most simply as x=f(4).
By the end you can
G8Find the inverse of a function where x appears twice, such as f(x) =2x+1x−3, by collecting the x terms and factorising.
G8Solve equations involving inverse and composite functions, such as f−1(x)=4 or f−1(x) =g(x).

x in two places

When x appears twice, the x terms are collected on one side. Then x is factorised out and freed by dividing.

Domain and range swap

The inverse swaps every input with its output. So the domain of f−1 is the range of f.

Use f, not the inverse

If f−1(x)=4, then x is the output of f at 4
So x=f(4), with no inverse needed.

Inverses inside equations
