Adding Fractions with Algebraic Denominators

Rates and times put letters in the denominator, as with two legs of a journey at different speeds or two pipes filling one tank. These fractions add by exactly the rule used for 13+14, with brackets in place of numbers.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Fractions with algebraic denominators add and subtract exactly as numerical ones do: over a common denominator, numerators only.
  • When two denominators share no factor, their product is the common denominator: ax+1+bx−2 =a(x−2)+b(x+1)(x+1)(x−2).
  • Each numerator is multiplied by the factor its own denominator is missing, and a subtracted numerator is bracketed so that every term changes sign.
  • A denominator that factorises is factorised first; the lowest common denominator uses each factor once, so x2−9 and x+3 give (x+3)(x−3).
  • The answer is usually left with a factorised denominator and is checked for a factor that cancels.

The same rule, with brackets

Fractions combine only over a common denominator. Two denominators with no common factor multiply to give it.

Fractions add or subtract only over a common denominator, whatever the denominators are made of. When two algebraic denominators share no factor, their product is the common denominator, just as 12 is for thirds and quarters.

Scale by the missing factor

Each numerator is multiplied by the factor its own denominator lacks. That is always the other fraction's denominator.

Each numerator is multiplied by the factor its own denominator is missing, so the top of each fraction gets the OTHER fraction's denominator. Scaling by its own denominator gives a different, wrong numerator.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Factorise the denominators first

Factorise every denominator first. The lowest common denominator uses each factor once.

A denominator that factorises can share a factor with the other denominator, and that is only visible once it is factorised. The lowest common denominator then uses each factor once, so x+3 and x2−9 =(x+3)(x−3) need only (x+3)(x−3).

Scale only what is missing

Each numerator gets only the factors its denominator lacks. A fraction already over the whole denominator stays as it is.

Each numerator is multiplied only by the factors its own denominator lacks. A fraction that already sits over the whole common denominator is left exactly as it is.

Answer2x−1(x+3)(x−3)

Answer2x−6x(x+2)(x−2)x+2 is in both denominators, so it appears once in the common denominator.
