Topic 11H.4 · Conditional and Algebraic Probability · Lesson 4 of 6

Counting Combinations of Outcomes

How many PINs, outfits or lunch menus are possible? Counting by multiplying is far faster than listing, and the count of all outcomes is the denominator of every equally likely probability.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The product rule for counting: if one choice can be made in m ways and a second in n ways, the two together can be made in m×n ways, and the rule extends to any number of choices.
  • Choosing from the same list again multiplies by the same number when repeats are allowed, and by one fewer each time when they are not.
  • A restriction is applied first to the place it controls, then the remaining places are counted from what is left.
  • When every outcome is equally likely, a probability is the count of wanted outcomes over the count of all outcomes.
By the end you can
G6Use the product rule to count the outcomes of two or more choices, with and without repeats.
G7Count outcomes with a restriction, such as an even last digit, and use the counts to find a probability.

Learn

Multiply the choices

One choice in m ways and another in n ways give m×n together. The rule extends to more choices.

If one choice can be made in m ways and a second in n ways, the two together can be made in m×n ways. Every main pairs with every dessert, so the pairs fill a grid, and the rule extends to three or more choices.

Repeats allowed, or not

With repeats, multiply by the same number again. Without, each later choice has one fewer option.

Choosing from the same list again multiplies by the same number when repeats are allowed. Without repeats each later choice has one fewer option, because one has been used.
WORKED EXAMPLE A
A café offers 3 starters, 5 mains and 4 desserts. How many different three-course meals are there?

Answer60 meals

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Fill the restricted place first

Fill the restricted place first. Count the other places from what is left.

A restriction is applied to its own place first, because it limits that place most. The other places are then counted from what is left.

Count, then divide

Probability = wanted count ÷ total count. Both counts come from the product rule.

When every outcome is equally likely, a probability is the count of wanted outcomes over the count of all outcomes. Both counts come from the product rule.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
How many 3-digit numbers can be made from the digits 1 to 7 with no repeats, if the number must be odd?

Then the first digit6 ways
Then the middle digit5 ways
Answer4×6×5=120
