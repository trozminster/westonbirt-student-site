The Factor Theorem

The factor theorem replaces a whole division with one substitution: if f(p)=0, then (x−p) is a factor. It is how cubics are factorised and solved, and how unknown coefficients are found, in almost every Pure paper.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The factor theorem: if f(p)=0, then (x−p) is a factor of f(x), and if (x−p) is a factor, then f(p)=0
  • For a factor (ax−b), test x=ba.
  • To factorise a cubic, test factors of its constant term until f(p)=0, then divide by (x−p) to find the quadratic factor.

A zero value means a factor

When f(p)=0, dividing f(x) by (x−p) leaves no remainder. So (x−p) is a factor, and if (x−p) is a factor, then f(p)=0

Test the value that makes the bracket 0

The factor (x−p) goes with x=p, so the sign flips. For a factor (ax−b), test x=ba.

Search the factors of the constant

A whole-number root of x3 +2x2 −5x −6 must divide 6
So test the factors of 6 until one gives 0

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

A factor gives an equation

If (x−p) is a factor of f(x), then f(p)=0
Substituting x=p turns an unknown coefficient into an equation to solve.

Two factors give two equations

Two unknowns need two facts. Each factor gives one equation, and the pair is solved simultaneously.
