Deriving the SUVAT Formulae

The constant acceleration formulae from chapter 9 are not separate facts. Integrating a constant acceleration produces them. Proving them, and knowing when the acceleration is constant enough to use them, decides the method in every mechanics question from here on.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Integrating a constant acceleration gives v=u+at; integrating again gives s =ut+12at2
  • The other constant acceleration formulae follow from these two by algebra.
  • Use the constant acceleration formulae only when the acceleration is constant; otherwise use calculus.

Integrate a constant acceleration

Integrating a constant acceleration a with respect to t gives v=∫adt =at+c. When t=0 the velocity is u, so c=u and v=u+at.

Integrate again for the displacement

Integrating v=u+at gives s = ∫ (u+at) dt =ut +12at2 +k. Measured from the starting point, s=0 when t=0, so k=0

The rest follow by algebra

Every other constant acceleration formula comes from these two. Substituting for u or eliminating t gives the remaining three.

A constant or a function of t

A constant acceleration lets you use the formulae, which are quicker. An acceleration that depends on t needs calculus.

Read what the question gives

A quantity written with t, such as v=3t2−2, means calculus. A number, or the words uniform acceleration, means the formulae.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
Find the distance a car travels as it accelerates uniformly from 4ms−1 to 20ms−1 in 8 seconds.
The acceleration is constant, so a formula is quicker than calculus.
