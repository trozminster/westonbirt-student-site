Motion in Two Dimensions

Forces in a real problem rarely line up. A boat is pushed by its engine, the wind and the current at once. Writing F=ma in i, j form finds the acceleration of anything moving in a plane, and the next lessons use the same law on bodies that are joined together.

By the end you’ll know…
  • F=ma holds for vectors: the acceleration has the same direction as the resultant force.
  • The magnitude of the acceleration is found by Pythagoras, and its direction with trigonometry, from its i and j components.
  • If a body accelerates parallel to a given vector, its acceleration and the resultant force are multiples of that vector.
By the end you can
Use F=ma with forces in i, j form to find the acceleration of a body, its magnitude and its direction.

F=ma works with vectors

With forces in i, j form, F=ma is a vector equation. Each component of the resultant is divided by the mass. The acceleration points the same way as the resultant force.

Size and direction of the acceleration

The magnitude of the acceleration comes from its components by Pythagoras, and its direction from the tangent of the angle. A sketch shows whether the angle is above or below i.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Work backwards for an unknown force

When the acceleration is known, ma gives the resultant force. Subtracting the known forces leaves the unknown one.

Parallel means a multiple

If a body accelerates parallel to a vector, its resultant force is a multiple λ of that vector. Comparing the i and j components finds λ and the unknown.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
A particle of mass 3 kg accelerates at (2𝐢+𝐣)ms−2 under the forces (4𝐢−𝐣) N and F N
find F.
ma is the resultant force
take off the force we know.
Find the resultant3(2𝐢+𝐣)=(6𝐢+3𝐣) N
Subtract the known forceF=(6𝐢+3𝐣) −(4𝐢−𝐣)
F=(2𝐢+4𝐣) N
WORKED EXAMPLE D
