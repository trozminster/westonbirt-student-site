Repeated Factors

A squared factor in the denominator needs one more partial fraction than it seems to. The method extends the last lesson's, and it is needed again for the binomial expansions of chapter 4

By the end you’ll know…
  • A repeated linear factor (x−a)2 in the denominator needs two partial fractions, Bx−a and Cx−a2
  • So a fraction with denominator (x−b)(x−a)2 is written as Ax−b +Bx−a +Cx−a2
  • Substituting the roots gives A and C; equate the coefficients of x2 (or substitute another value of x) to find B.
By the end you can
Split a fraction with a repeated linear factor in its denominator into three partial fractions, such as 11x2+14x+5(x+1)2(2x+1).
Factorise a cubic denominator such as x3−2x2+x fully, decide the form of the partial fractions, and find the constants.

Two fractions for a squared factor

A repeated factor (x−a)2 needs Bx−a and Cx−a2
Adding 2x+1 and 3x+12 shows why: both sit over (x+1)2 once combined.

The identity

Multiply through by (x+1)2(2x+1). Each term keeps only the factors its own denominator lacks.

Two roots, then a coefficient

The roots x=−1 and x = −½ give B and C. Equating the x2 coefficients, 11=2A+C, gives A.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Factorise fully first

A cubic denominator must be fully factorised before you choose the form. x3−2x2+x =x(x−1)2 has a repeated factor.

Read the form from the factors

Each distinct linear factor gets one fraction, and a squared factor gets two. The number of constants equals the degree of the denominator.

The factor theorem

With no common factor, test small values of x. If f(1)=0, then (x−1) is a factor.
