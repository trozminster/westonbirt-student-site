Methods of Proof

Some statements are proved by checking every case, and a single counter-example is enough to show that a statement is false. These finish the chapter's methods of proof, which later chapters use whenever a question says prove or show that.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Proof by exhaustion splits a statement into a finite number of cases, such as n even and n odd, and proves each case.
  • A single counter-example is enough to disprove a statement.
  • A proof by exhaustion must cover every case, so state the cases before proving them.
By the end you can
Prove a statement by exhaustion, splitting it into cases and proving each one.
Disprove a statement by finding a counter-example, and state why it fails.

Split into cases

Proof by exhaustion splits every possibility into a finite number of cases. Each case is proved, so together they cover everything.

State the cases first

Name the cases before proving them, and check that they cover every value. A missing case leaves the proof unfinished.

WORKED EXAMPLE A
Prove, by considering n even and n odd, that n2+3n is even for every integer n.
Every integer is even or odd, so the two cases n=2k and n=2k+1 cover them all.
Take n=2kn2+3n=4k2+6k
Factor out 22(2k2+3k), even
Take n=2k+1n2+3n=4k2+10k+4
Factor out 22(2k2+5k+2), even
Concludeeven in every case
WORKED EXAMPLE B
Prove that n2+1 is not a multiple of 3 for 1≤n≤6
There are only six cases, so check every one.
List every casen=1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
Find each value2, 5, 10, 17, 26, 37
Concludenone is a multiple of 3

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

One counter-example disproves

A statement about every n is false if a single value breaks it. The claim that 2n−1 is prime for every n≥2 fails at n=4

Show why it fails

A counter-example must meet the statement's conditions, and it needs its working and a reason. Work out the value, then say why it breaks the statement.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
Disprove: n2+n+1 is prime for every positive integer n.
n=1, 2 and 3 give 3, 7 and 13, all prime, so keep going.
Try n=442+4+1=21
Explain21=3×7, not prime
Concludethe statement is false
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Disprove: (x+1)2≥x2 for every real number x.
Positive values all worktry a negative one.
Try x=−2(−2+1)2=1
(−2)2=4
Explain1<4, so (x+1)2<x2
Concludethe statement is false
