Topic 10.2 · Arithmetic Sequences · Lesson 1 of 3

Generating Sequences and Common Differences

A sequence that changes by the same amount at every step is the simplest pattern in mathematics, and it is the one the rest of this half-term is built on - the nth term rule next lesson, and the straight-line graph in Topic 3, are the same idea written two other ways.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A term-to-term rule gives the next term from the previous one; a position-to-term (nth term) rule gives any term directly from its position. An arithmetic sequence changes by the same amount, its common difference, each time - found by subtracting any term from the one after it. A negative common difference means the sequence decreases.

Two ways to describe a sequence

A term-to-term rule reaches each term from the one before. A position-to-term rule, the nth term, uses the position n. So any term can be reached directly.

A sequence can be described in two different ways, and which description you are handed decides how much work a single term costs. A term-to-term rule says how each term is reached from the one before it, so the 50th term can only be reached by passing through the 49 in front of it. A position-to-term rule - the nth term rule - is written in terms of the position number n itself, so any term can be reached directly from its position, with no earlier terms needed. Both describe the same sequence; they differ only in what they take as their starting point.

The common difference

In an arithmetic sequence, the change between terms is always the same: the common difference, d. A negative d means the sequence decreases. So 30−4n starts at 30−4=26, not 30, and falls by 4

What makes a sequence arithmetic is that the change from one term to the next is the same every time. That constant amount is the common difference, d, and because it is constant it can be recovered from any adjacent pair - the later term minus the earlier one, in that order, because the order of the subtraction is what carries the sign. A positive d means the sequence increases; a negative d means it decreases, and a decreasing sequence is no less arithmetic for it.

Recovering the common difference from the sequence itself

Find d by subtracting each term from the next. Always take the later term minus the earlier one. The other order gives the wrong sign.

Most of the time a sequence arrives as a list of numbers with no rule attached, and the common difference has to be recovered from the terms themselves. Subtracting one term from the next gives it, and the direction of that subtraction is the whole of the difficulty: it is always the later term minus the earlier one. Reversing the order gives the right size with the wrong sign, which turns a decreasing sequence into an increasing one on paper while the numbers on the page still go down.

When the terms given are not next to each other

For terms further apart, count the steps between their positions. From the 2nd to the 5th term is three steps, not four. Divide the total change by the steps.

A common difference can still be recovered when the two terms given are further apart, but the gap between them is then several steps rather than one, and the total change has to be shared between those steps. The number of steps is the difference between the two positions, not the number of terms - between the 2nd term and the 5th there are three steps, not four. The total change divided by the number of steps is the common difference.
