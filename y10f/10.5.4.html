Topic 10.5 · Area and Perimeter · Lesson 4 of 4

Missing Lengths and Inverse Problems

Measuring a shape you can see is only half the skill. A designer far more often knows how much material there is and needs the dimension that will use it, which is the same formula read in the opposite direction.

By the end you’ll know…
  • An area or perimeter formula can be applied backwards, using inverse operations, to find a missing length: for a triangle, base =2 × area / height; for a rectangle or parallelogram, a missing side is the area divided by the known side; for a circle, the radius is found from the area by dividing by π and taking the square root, or from the circumference by dividing by 2×π. Where a length appears more than once, or the shape is compound, the situation is written as an equation and solved.

02

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

A formula read backwards

A known area is taken back through the formula in reverse. Each operation is undone by its inverse, in the opposite order. A parallelogram has nothing to halve, so its base is its area ÷ its height.

An area formula is a chain of operations applied to the lengths of a shape, so a known area can be taken back through that chain in reverse to recover a length. The operations are undone in the opposite order to the one in which they were applied, and each is replaced by its inverse: multiplying becomes dividing, halving becomes doubling, and squaring becomes taking the square root. Nothing new is being learned about the shape here; what changes is only which quantity is known and which is wanted. The safest way to keep the order straight is to write the forward formula down first and then unwind it one step at a time.

Where perimeter differs from area

A perimeter adds sides, so a missing side is found by subtracting the known ones. Divide only what is left, and only between equal sides. A circumference is built by multiplying, so a radius is C÷(2×π).

A missing length recovered from a perimeter is found by subtraction rather than by division, because a perimeter adds the sides rather than multiplying them. The known sides are totalled and taken away from the perimeter, and only then, if two equal sides remain unknown, is the remainder divided. Dividing the perimeter by the number of sides at the start assumes every side is the same length, which is true only for a regular shape and is the commonest wrong first move. Area and perimeter are inverted by different operations because they were built by different ones.

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Working back through several parts

Narrow the total area to the one part holding the unknown. Subtract the parts you can calculate, then invert that part's own formula.

When a compound shape's total area is known and a length inside it is not, the total is first reduced to the part that contains the unknown. The areas of the parts that can be calculated are found and subtracted from the total, leaving the area of the one remaining piece, and only that piece's own formula is then inverted. The order matters: the whole figure has no single formula to invert, so nothing can be done until the problem has been narrowed to one part. This is the same splitting habit used for a forward area, run in the other direction.

When the unknown appears twice

A length appearing twice cannot be isolated by subtraction. Write the figure as an equation in that length and solve it.

Sometimes a length appears in more than one place in a figure, so subtracting the known parts does not isolate it. The situation is then written as an equation in that length and solved, exactly as any other equation is. Writing the equation is the work; solving it uses nothing new. A shape whose two dimensions are described in terms of one another is the same case, and so is a perimeter in which several sides are given as multiples of one unknown side. The signal to reach for an equation is that the unknown will not come out by a single division.

A perimeter is built by adding the sides, so it is unwound by subtracting the known ones; an area is built by multiplying two lengths, so it is unwound by dividing by the length that is known.
