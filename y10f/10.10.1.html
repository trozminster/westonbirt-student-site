Writing Numbers in Standard Form

The Sun is about 150,000,000 km away and a red blood cell is about 0.000008 m across. Standard form writes both in a few digits, so they can be read and compared at a glance.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A number is in standard form when it is written as a × 10ⁿ, where a is at least 1 and less than 10, and n is a whole number.
  • A positive power of 10 makes the number larger and a negative power makes it smaller, so 1.5 × 10⁸ = 150,000,000 and 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 0.0015.
  • The power counts the places the decimal point moves, not the number of zeros written.
  • A power can also be rewritten with a different base, because (aᵐ)ⁿ = a⁽ᵐ × ⁿ⁾.
By the end you can
G4Write an ordinary number in standard form, and write a number given in standard form as an ordinary number, for both positive and negative powers.
G4Rewrite a power with a different base using the power law, for example writing 8⁴ as 2¹².

One digit before the point

Standard form is a × 10ⁿ, with a at least 1 and less than 10. So 4.5 × 10⁶ is in standard form. 45 × 10⁵ is not.

A number in standard form is a × 10ⁿ, where a is at least 1 and less than 10. So 4.5 × 10⁶ is in standard form, but 45 × 10⁵ is not.

The power counts the places

The power counts the places the decimal point moves, not the zeros written. A positive power makes a large number and a negative power makes a small one.

A power of a power

(2³)⁴ is four lots of 2³ multiplied together. So the indices multiply: (2³)⁴ = 2¹². In general, (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ.

(2³)⁴ means 2³ used as a factor four times, so the indices add to 3 × 4 = 12. In general (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ.

Writing the base as a power

A base can be rewritten as a power of a smaller base. Since 8 = 2³, 8⁴ = (2³)⁴ = 2¹².

A power can take a new base when the old base is a power of it. Since 8 = 2³, 8⁴ = (2³)⁴ = 2¹².
