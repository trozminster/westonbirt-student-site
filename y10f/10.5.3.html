Topic 10.5 · Area and Perimeter · Lesson 3 of 4

Compound Shapes and Unit Conversion

Real objects are rarely one clean shape, and the lengths on a plan are rarely all in the same unit. A floor quoted in square metres and measured in centimetres is a single problem, and getting it wrong by a factor of a hundred is the commonest way to lose an answer that was otherwise right.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A compound shape is split into rectangles, triangles, parallelograms, trapezia and circular parts whose areas are added, or treated as a larger shape with a piece subtracted; only the outside edges count towards the perimeter. Converting an area between units squares the conversion factor, so 1 m squared =10,000 cm squared, not 100 cm squared.

Learn

Two routes to the same area

Add the pieces, or subtract a piece from a surrounding shape. Both give the same area, so choose the route with fewer unknown lengths.

A compound shape is handled by turning it into shapes that already have formulas, and there are always two ways to do that. It can be split into pieces whose areas are added, or it can be seen as a larger surrounding shape with a piece taken away. Both are correct and both give the same total, because area does not depend on how a figure is cut up. Choosing between them is a practical decision: the better route is the one that needs fewer unknown lengths to be worked out first. Doing it both ways is also the cheapest check available, since two independent routes agreeing is strong evidence and two routes disagreeing locates the error.

A missing length is deduced

A missing length is deduced from the lengths given, because opposite sides must agree. Read the figure to see whether it is a sum or a difference.

A plan of a compound shape usually gives fewer lengths than there are edges, and the rest are recovered from the ones given rather than from the drawing. The reasoning is that opposite sides of the surrounding rectangle must agree, so a length running one way must equal the total of the lengths running the same way on the other side of the figure. Whether a missing length is a sum or a difference of two given ones depends on which direction it runs and where it sits, and reading that from the figure is the step to slow down for. A drawing is not to scale, so an eye is no substitute for the arithmetic.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

An area conversion squares its factor

An area conversion uses the length factor twice, so 1 m² is 10000 cm²
The factor belongs to the dimension, not to the pair of units.

Converting a length from metres to centimetres multiplies by 100, because that is how many centimetres fit along one metre. Converting an area multiplies by 100 twice, once for each direction the shape extends in, so the factor is 10000 and not 100
The same reasoning gives 1 cm² =100 mm² and 1 km² =1000000 m²
A conversion factor therefore belongs to a dimension, not to a pair of units, and using the length factor on an area is not a small error - it is wrong by the factor itself, which here is a hundredfold.

Convert first or last, but decide

Every length in one calculation must be in the same unit. Convert them all first, or convert the answer at the end.

A problem whose lengths arrive in mixed units cannot be calculated as it stands, because an area formula multiplies two lengths and both must be measured in the same unit. There are two safe orders. Everything can be converted to one unit before any calculation, which is usually the simpler discipline. Or the calculation can be done in the units given and the answer converted at the end, which is only safe when every given length was already in the same unit. What is never safe is converting some lengths and not others, and the check is to look back at the original figure once an answer is reached and ask what unit each number on it was in.
