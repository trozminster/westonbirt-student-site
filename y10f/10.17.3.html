Factorising into Two Brackets

Factorising turns a quadratic back into the brackets that made it. It is the key that opens quadratic equations next lesson.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Factorising reverses expanding: x2+bx+c factorises as (x+p)(x+q) where p+q=b and pq=c.
  • So the two numbers are found by listing the factor pairs of c and choosing the pair that adds to b.
  • If c is positive both numbers share the sign of b; if c is negative they have opposite signs.
  • A common factor in every term is taken out first, because 2x2+10x+12 =2(x2+5x+6).

Factorising runs the grid backwards

(x+2)(x+5) expands to x2+7x+10
Factorising undoes that. The two numbers add to make the 7 and multiply to make the 10

List the factor pairs of c

List the factor pairs of the constant. Choose the pair that adds to the x coefficient. For x2+7x+12, that is 3 and 4

The factor pairs of the constant are listed, and the pair that adds to the x coefficient is chosen. For x2+7x+12, only 3 and 4 add to 7

The signs follow the constant

If the constant is positive, both numbers share the sign of the x term. If it is negative, the two numbers have opposite signs.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

A common factor comes out first

When every term shares a factor, it is taken out before anything else. Then the bracket left behind is factorised as usual.

Check by expanding

A factorisation is checked by expanding it back to the original. If the expansion is different, a sign or a pair is wrong.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
Factorise 3x2+21x+30
Write the expression3x2+21x+30
Take out the 3=3(x2+7x+10)The pair is 2 and 5: 2+5=7 and 2×5=10
Factorise the bracket=3(x+2)(x+5)
