Topic 10.22 · Direct and Inverse Proportion · Lesson 1 of 5

Recognising Direct Proportion

Some quantities grow in step, like pay and hours worked, and some only look as if they do. Today we test for direct proportion and use it.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Quantities in direct proportion satisfy y=kx for a fixed number k, so y÷x is the same for every pair.
  • The graph of a direct proportion is a straight line through the origin, and k is its gradient.
  • A straight line that does NOT pass through the origin is not a direct proportion, however regular it looks.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

A fixed multiplier

Quantities in direct proportion satisfy y=kx for a fixed number k. So the output divided by the input is the same for every pair.

A line through the origin

The graph of a direct proportion is a straight line through the origin. Its gradient is the multiplier k.

A line that misses the origin

A straight line that misses the origin is not direct proportion. A taxi fare of £3 plus £2 a mile is one.

A straight line that misses the origin is not a direct proportion, however regular it looks. A taxi fare of £3 plus £2 a mile is one.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Use k for a missing value

Find k from one pair, then use y=kx. If y=10 when x=4, k=2.5
So x=8 gives y=20

Once k is known, any missing value comes from y=kx. If y=10 when x=4, then k=2.5, and x=8 gives y=20

The multiplier in context

In context, k is a rate, such as £ per kilogram. Its units say which quantity it multiplies.

In context, k is a rate, such as a price per kilogram or pay per hour. Its units say which quantity it multiplies.
