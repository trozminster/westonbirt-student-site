Sharing in a Given Ratio

Sharing a bill, mixing a concrete, splitting winnings and scaling a recipe are all the same calculation, and it is one that goes wrong most often at the very first step: counting how many equal parts there are to share into.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A ratio can be read as a total number of equal parts to be shared out: the total divided by the number of parts gives the value of one part, and each share is that part value multiplied by its own number of parts.

02

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Count the parts before dividing

Divide the quantity by the total number of parts, not by the number of shares. Each share is then one part times its own number of parts.

A ratio can be read as a total number of equal parts, so the quantity to be shared is divided by that total and not by the number of shares. In the ratio 3:2 there are five parts, not two, and dividing by two would give half the quantity to each side regardless of what the ratio said. Once the value of one part is known, each share is that value multiplied by its own number of parts, which is why the shares come out unequal from an equal division. Adding the shares back together and checking they give the original total is a complete check on the whole calculation.

Every share is a multiple of one part

Only the value of one part needs calculating. Each share is that value times its own number of parts. The larger side gets the larger share.

The value of one part is the only number that has to be calculated; each share is then built from it by multiplying. This is what keeps the shares in the right proportion and what makes a three-part ratio no harder than a two-part one. It also explains why a share is never the value of one part unless that side of the ratio happens to be 1
Reading the answer back against the ratio - the larger number of parts must have the larger share - catches the error of pairing the shares with the wrong sides.

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

When the difference is given

A given difference is worth (a−b) parts, not the whole total. Divide it by (a−b) to reach the value of one part.

A ratio problem does not always start from the total. If only the difference between two shares is known, that difference is not the whole quantity and must not be divided by the total number of parts. The difference between the shares in a:b is (a−b) parts, so dividing the given difference by (a−b) is what gives the value of one part. Everything after that is unchanged: each share is one part multiplied by its own number of parts, and the total, if it is wanted, is one part multiplied by (a+b). Deciding which number of parts the given quantity corresponds to is the whole of the new work.

Reading which quantity has been given

A given single share is worth its own number of parts. Write down what the given number counts, in parts, before dividing.

The same reasoning covers the case where the value of one share alone is given: that share is its own number of parts, so dividing by that number gives one part. Three different givens - the total, the difference and one share - therefore lead to three different divisors, and every one of them is read off the ratio rather than assumed. Writing down what the given number counts, in parts, before dividing anything is the habit that prevents the error, and it makes all three cases the same problem.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Money is shared in the ratio 5:3, and one share is £40 more than the other. Find both shares.
Subtract the parts5−3=2 parts
Match the difference2 parts=£40
1 part=£20
Multiply each part£100 and £60
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Sweets are shared in the ratio 7:4, with 32 in the smaller share. How many are there in total?
Match the share4 parts=32
1 part=8
Add the parts7+4=11
Find the total11×8=88 sweets
