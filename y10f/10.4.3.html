Two-Way Tables

A Venn diagram sorts things by which sets they belong to. A two-way table sorts them by two characteristics at once - year group against how they travel, say - and because every person lands in exactly one cell, the same counting that answered Venn questions answers these.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A two-way table organises two characteristics of a group at once, one across the columns and one down the rows, with row and column totals and a grand total that must all be self-consistent.

What the structure guarantees

Every member falls into exactly one inner cell. Each margin total is the sum of its own row or column.

A two-way table splits a group by one characteristic across its columns and another down its rows, so every member of the group falls into exactly one inner cell. The margins are what make the table more than a grid of numbers: each row total is the sum of that row's cells, each column total is the sum of that column's, and the grand total in the corner is reachable by adding either the row totals or the column totals. Those two routes to the same number are the table's own check on itself.

Why a missing cell is never a guess

A missing cell comes from its row total or its column total. Both routes must give the same value.

Because a cell sits in exactly one row and one column, it is fixed by either of them: its row total minus the rest of its row, or its column total minus the rest of its column. Both routes must give the same value, and that is what turns a completed table from a plausible one into a correct one. Filling a cell from one total and never testing it against the other is where an error survives, because a single wrong entry still makes one margin add up.
COMPLETING A TWO-WAY TABLE
A group of 80 students is split by year group and by whether they study French. 22 of the 45 Year 9 students study French, and 30 students in total study French. Complete the two-way table.
Set the table outFrench and Not French across the columns, Year 9 and Year 10 down the rows, with totals on both margins.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Which total the question means

Chosen from everyone, the denominator is the grand total. Restricted to one row or column, it is that total instead.

A probability from a two-way table is a cell count over a total, and the whole difficulty is which total. When the question is about the group as a whole - a person chosen at random from everyone - the denominator is the grand total. When the question restricts attention to part of the group - chosen from the Year 10 students, or from those who take the bus - the denominator is that row's or that column's total, because the restriction has changed what is possible. The numerator is the same cell either way; only what counts as possible has moved.

A probability, not a count

A count is not yet a probability. Write the cell over the total it belongs to, then convert if the question asks.

A cell count is not yet an answer. A probability is a proportion, so the count has to be written over the total it belongs to and then, if the question asks, converted to a decimal or a percentage. Leaving the answer as a bare count says nothing about how likely anything is, because the same count means different things against different totals - 12 out of 30 and 12 out of 55 are the same cell and two different probabilities.
RESTRICTING TO A ROW
A two-way table of 55 students has Year 10 walking 12 and taking the bus 18 (row total 30), and Year 11 walking 15 and taking the bus 10 (row total 25). Find the probability that a student chosen at random walks
then the probability that a Year 10 student chosen at random walks.
Step 1: For the first question the choice is from everyone, so the denominator is the grand total, 55. Step 2: The walkers are 12 + 15 = 27, the column total. So P(walks) = 2755.
Step 3: For the second question the choice is restricted to Year 10, so the denominator is that row's total, 30. Step 4: The Year 10 walkers are the single cell 12. So P(walks, given Year 10) = 1230 = 25.
Compare them2755 is about 0.49 and 25 is 0.4. The same table, two different questions, two different answers - and the restriction is what changed the denominator.
TO A COLUMN
Now one asked the other way round, restricting to a column. Using the same table, find the probability that a student who takes the bus is in Year 11.
Step 1: The restriction is to bus users, so the denominator is the bus column total: 18 + 10 = 28.
The numerator is the cell where bus meets Year 1110.
Step 3: P(Year 11, given bus) = 1028 = 514. Note this is not the same as P(bus, given Year 11), which would be 1025 - the same cell over a different restriction.
YOUR TURN · b
A two-way table of 90 people has adults liking tea 24 and coffee 26 (row total 50), and children liking tea 28 and coffee 12 (row total 40). Find P(tea) for a person chosen at random, then for a child chosen at random. Then find P(adult) for a tea-drinker chosen at random, saying which total you divided by each time and why.
tea drinkers are 24 + 28 = 52, so P(tea) = 5290 = 2645, divided by the grand total because the choice was from everyone. Exit ticket

On paper, on your own, then check
1
Explain what the row totals, the column totals and the grand total of a two-way table have to satisfy, and why that gives two ways to find a missing cell.
Each row and column sums to its total, and both margins sum to the grand total. So a missing cell can be found from its row or its column, and the two must agree.
2

Both 9, cycle only 13, drive only 21, neither 7
