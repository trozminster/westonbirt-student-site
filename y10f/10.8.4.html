Forming Equations from Contexts

Nobody hands you an equation in real life. You are handed a situation. Turning the situation into an equation is the step that makes the algebra useful.

By the end you’ll know…
  • An equation is formed by naming the unknown with a letter, writing every other quantity in terms of that letter, and then using a fact that makes two expressions equal.
  • In a geometric diagram that fact is usually an angle sum - 180° on a line or in a triangle, 360° at a point or in a quadrilateral - or a perimeter given as a total, or two sides known to be equal.
  • What the letter stands for is written down before the equation is formed, including its units.
  • The value of the letter is not always the answer to the question, so the question is read again once the equation is solved.

Name the unknown first

Name the unknown with a letter. Write every other quantity in terms of it. A fact that makes two things equal gives the equation.

An equation is formed by naming the unknown with a letter and writing every other quantity in terms of it. A fact that makes two things equal then gives the equation.

The letter is not always the answer

Solving gives the value of the letter. Reading the question again shows whether that value, or something built from it, was asked for.

An angle sum makes the equation

In a diagram, the equal fact is often an angle sum or a perimeter. Add the expressions and set them equal to that total.

In a diagram the equal fact is usually an angle sum, a perimeter given as a total, or two sides that are equal. The expressions are added and set equal to that total.

Perimeter counts every side

A rectangle's perimeter is twice the length plus twice the width. It is never just length plus width.

A rectangle's perimeter is twice its length plus twice its width, never length plus width. So a rectangle x+3 long and x wide has perimeter 2(x+3) +2x.
