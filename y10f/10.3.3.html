Writing the Equation of a Line

Finding an nth term rule never needed the terms listed out - the common difference and the first term were enough. The same economy is available for a straight line: given its gradient and its y-intercept, the equation can be written down, with no table of values anywhere.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Just as an arithmetic sequence's common difference and first term give its nth-term rule directly (dn + c) without listing every term, a straight line's gradient and y-intercept give its equation directly (y = mx + c) without plotting a table of points.

Learn

Two numbers are enough

A line is fixed by its gradient and its y-intercept. Build the equation from those two numbers, with no table of values.

An arithmetic sequence's rule is built directly from two facts about it, the common difference and the first term, and no term ever has to be listed. A straight line works the same way. Its gradient and its y-intercept determine it completely, so the equation can be assembled from those two numbers alone. A table of values is one way of finding points on a line; it is not a step the equation requires, and reaching for it when both numbers are already known is work that the structure has already done.

Where each number goes, and why

The gradient multiplies x. The intercept is added on by itself. Swapping them gives a different line.

The gradient is attached to x and the intercept stands alone, and the reason is what each one does. The gradient is a rate: it applies once for every unit of x, so it must be multiplied by x. The intercept is a fixed amount that does not depend on x at all, so it is added on its own. Swapping them produces an equation that describes a different line entirely, and the arithmetic gives no warning, because both arrangements look equally reasonable on the page.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The gradient from two points

Two points read off the line give both changes. Divide the change in y by the change in x.

A gradient is a comparison of two changes, and any two points read off the drawn line supply both of them. The change in y between the points is the vertical change; the change in x between the same two points is the horizontal change; and the gradient is the first divided by the second. Choosing two points where the line crosses the grid lines makes both changes whole numbers, which is what turns counting squares into an exact calculation.

Keeping the subtractions in step

Subtract both coordinates in the same order. Keep the vertical change on top, never the other way round.

The two subtractions must run in the same direction through the pair of points. Taking the second point's y minus the first point's y, and the second point's x minus the first point's x, gives a correct gradient; so does doing both the other way round, because the two sign changes cancel. Mixing them is what goes wrong: one subtraction reversed and the other not leaves the size right and the sign wrong, which turns a falling line into a rising one on paper. And the quotient is the vertical change over the horizontal, never the reverse - inverting it describes a different steepness entirely.
