Circumference and Area of Circles

A circle has no straight sides to measure, so neither of the rules built up so far will reach it. Every wheel, pipe, plate and roundabout is sized by two formulas that between them describe the one shape a ruler alone cannot handle.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The circumference of a circle is C=π×d (or C=2×π×r) and its area is A=π×r squared; the radius is squared before multiplying by π, since the index applies only to r.A semicircle's area is half the full circle's area, but its perimeter is half the circumference plus the diameter - the straight edge must be added separately.

One constant, two formulas

Every circle's circumference ÷ diameter is the same number, π. Circumference is π×d. Area is π×r2

Every circle, whatever its size, has a circumference about 3.14 times its diameter, and that fixed ratio is what pi names. Because the ratio never changes, one measurement is enough to describe a whole circle: from the diameter the circumference follows as π x d, and from the radius the area follows as π x r2
The two formulas are not interchangeable and they do not measure the same thing - one is a length around the edge and the other is a covering of the inside, which is why one answer is in centimetres and the other in square centimetres. Reading the units of an answer is therefore a check on which formula was used.

The index belongs to the radius alone

The index sits on the radius alone, so square r before multiplying by π. Squaring the diameter gives an answer four times too large.

In the area formula the index sits on the r and nowhere else, so the radius is squared first and the result is then multiplied by π. Two errors follow from missing that. Squaring the diameter instead of the radius gives an answer four times too large, because doubling a length quadruples the square. Multiplying by π before squaring changes the order of two operations that do not commute, so the answer is wrong by a factor of π. The safest habit is to write down the radius as its own line before the formula is touched at all.

Half a circle is not half of everything

A semicircle has half the area of its circle. Its perimeter is half the circumference plus the diameter. The cut makes a new straight edge.

A semicircle's area is exactly half the full circle's area, because cutting a shape in two halves the space it covers. Its perimeter is not half the circumference, because the cut has created a new edge that the full circle never had. The straight edge is the diameter, and it must be added on: half the circumference plus the diameter. Area and perimeter therefore respond differently to the same cut, which is the whole difficulty of the shape. A quarter circle behaves the same way, with a quarter of the area and two radii added to a quarter of the circumference.

Circular parts inside a bigger figure

Each part of a joined figure is found by its own formula. Only edges on the outside count towards the perimeter.

When a circle or semicircle is joined to a straight-sided shape, the same splitting habit applies: each part is found by its own formula and the parts are combined. What changes is that a curved edge may be swallowed by the join, or a straight edge may be. Only the edges that lie on the outside of the finished figure count towards its perimeter, so a semicircle sitting on a rectangle contributes its arc to the perimeter but not its diameter, since the diameter has become the join. Deciding which edges survive the join is the step worth doing on the drawing before any calculation begins.

1
Explain why a semicircle's area is half a circle's area but its perimeter is not half a circle's circumference.
Halving the shape halves the space it covers, so the area halves exactly; but cutting the circle in two creates a straight edge along the diameter that the circle never had, so the perimeter is half the circumference plus the diameter.
