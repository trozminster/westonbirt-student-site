Negative and Zero Indices

A negative index looks as though it ought to produce a negative answer, and it never does. It is the notation the whole of standard form rests on, and it is the one place where reading the symbol literally leads consistently in the wrong direction.

A negative index flips the power

The pattern of powers carries on below an index of 0 into fractions. So a⁻ⁿ is 1aⁿ, and the value stays positive.

The pattern of powers of a base falls by a factor of that base at every step down, and continuing the pattern past an index of 0 does not stop at 0 or turn negative: it carries on into fractions. So a⁻ⁿ means 1aⁿ, the reciprocal of the positive power, and the answer is always positive when the base is. The minus sign is instruction about where the power goes, not about the sign of the value. Nothing here is special to base 10; the pattern is the same for every base, which is what this lesson adds to the standard-form work already met.

The rule works for every base

The rule belongs to the index, not to base 10. 5⁻² is 125 by the same reasoning that makes 10⁻² equal 1100.

Powers of 10 with negative indices are met first because standard form needs them, and that can leave the impression that the rule belongs to base 10. It does not. 5⁻² is 125 by exactly the reasoning that makes 10⁻² equal to 1100, and the same holds for a fraction or a letter as the base. Recognising that is what allows a negative index to be used inside the multiplication and division laws, since those laws never mentioned the base either.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

One base, three kinds of index

Positive, zero and negative indices are one pattern, not three rules. Each step down the powers divides by the base.

Positive, zero and negative indices of the same base are three parts of a single pattern, not three separate rules. Reading the powers of a base downwards, each step divides by the base: the values fall through the whole numbers, arrive at 1 when the index is 0, and continue into fractions below it. Seeing the pattern as continuous is what makes a mixed expression manageable, because the multiplication and division laws then apply across all three kinds without any special cases. The indices are added or subtracted exactly as before, with their signs.

Adding indices with their signs

Indices are added and subtracted with their signs, like any directed numbers. Subtracting a negative index increases the total.

When an expression mixes positive, negative and zero indices of one base, the work is arithmetic with directed numbers on the indices. Adding a negative index reduces the total; subtracting one increases it, because subtracting a negative adds. A zero index contributes nothing to the sum, which is another way of saying that a⁰ is 1. Doing that arithmetic carefully is the whole of the skill, and the commonest error is a sign lost when a negative index is subtracted rather than the index rule itself being misremembered.
