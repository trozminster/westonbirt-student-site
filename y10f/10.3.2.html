Gradient and y-intercept

Two numbers describe a straight line completely, and they are the same two that describe an arithmetic sequence: a constant step and an anchor. On a graph they are called the gradient and the y-intercept, and once you can read them off, the line and its equation become interchangeable.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In y=mx+c, m is the gradient (steepness: vertical change divided by horizontal change) and c is the y-intercept, the value of y where the line crosses the y-axis (when x=0).

What the two numbers measure

The gradient m is the vertical change divided by the horizontal change. The intercept c is the value of y when x is 0

A straight line is fixed by how steeply it rises and by where it sits. The gradient, m, measures the steepness as a comparison: the vertical change divided by the horizontal change between any two points on the line. It is a ratio, not a count of squares, and because the line is straight the same ratio comes out wherever it is measured. The y-intercept, c, fixes the line's position - it is the value of y at the moment x is 0, so it is where the line meets the vertical axis, and no other crossing point tells you c.

Why the axes have to be read before anything is counted

Read the scale on each axis before counting anything. A line falling from left to right has a negative gradient.

Counting squares is a shortcut that only works when one square means one unit on both axes, and on real graphs it often does not. What the gradient compares is the change in the quantities, not the number of squares crossed, so the scale on each axis has to be read first and the changes taken in units. The direction has to be read too: a line falling from left to right has a negative vertical change against a positive horizontal one, so its gradient is negative, however many squares it crosses.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The equation already contains both numbers

In y=mx+c the number multiplying x is the gradient. The number added on is the y-intercept.

When an equation is written as y=mx+c, no reading off a graph is needed at all: the number multiplying x is the gradient and the number added on is the y-intercept, in that order and never the other way round. The order matters because the two do different jobs - one is a rate, attached to x because it applies once for every unit of x, and the other is a fixed amount that does not depend on x at all, which is why it stands alone.

Sketching from the two numbers

Plot the y-intercept, then step one across and m up. Starting at the origin instead sketches a different line.

A sketch does not need a table of values. The y-intercept gives one point outright, on the vertical axis, and the gradient gives the direction to travel from it: for every one unit across, m units up when m is positive and m units down when it is negative. Two points determine a line, so the intercept and one step are enough. The intercept is the part most often lost - a line drawn from the origin is a sketch of a different equation, one whose c is zero.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Find the gradient and y-intercept of y=3x−4, then sketch it.
m=3 and c=−4, taking the sign with it. Mark (0,−4), go 1 across and 3 up to (1,−1), then draw the line through both and extend it.
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Find the gradient and y-intercept of y=6−2x, then sketch it.
Rewrite it as y=−2x+6: m=−2 and c=6, even though the 6 was written first. Mark (0,6), go 1 across and 2 down to (1,4), then draw the line, which falls.
