Topic 10.2 · Arithmetic Sequences · Lesson 2 of 3

Finding the nth Term Rule

Listing terms one at a time is fine for the 5th term and hopeless for the 500th. The nth term rule is what removes the listing altogether, and it is built from two numbers you already know how to find.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The nth term rule of an arithmetic sequence has the form dn + c, where d is the common difference and c is found by working back one step from the first term (c = first term - d). Substituting a position number into the rule generates that term directly, without working out every term before it.

Where dn + c comes from

Every arithmetic sequence has a rule dn + c. Its terms sit c away from the multiples of d. So c is the first term minus d.

Every arithmetic sequence has a rule of the same shape, and knowing the shape is most of the work. Because the sequence changes by d at every step, its terms sit a fixed distance from the multiples of d - the sequence you would get if the rule were dn on its own. That fixed distance is c, and working back one step from the first term finds it, because position 0 is one step before position 1: c is the first term minus d.

What the rule buys you

With the rule, a term needs only its position. Substitute n, with no earlier terms needed. The 100th term costs what the 3rd costs.

Once the rule is written, a term's position is all that is needed to produce it: substituting n gives that term without generating a single one of the terms before it. That is the whole point of a position-to-term rule - the 100th term costs exactly what the 3rd costs.

Learn

A common difference need not be a whole number

The common difference d need not be a whole number. A sequence going up by 0.5 each step is still arithmetic. Only the change must stay the same.

Nothing in the rule dn + c requires d to be a whole number. A sequence can change by 1.5, or by a quarter, at every step and it is arithmetic in exactly the same sense, because the only condition is that the change is the same every time. Whether the terms themselves come out whole is a separate question from whether the difference does, and running the two together is what makes these sequences look stranger than they are.

Building and reading the rule when d is not whole

Build the rule exactly as before: c is the first term minus d. Either may be a fraction or a decimal. A coefficient like 12 multiplies n, like any other.

The rule is built exactly as before and only the arithmetic changes. d is still the common difference and c is still the first term minus d, but either may now be a fraction or a decimal. Substituting a position then means multiplying that position by d, so a coefficient like 12 is a multiplication like any other and not a separate instruction to halve something afterwards - the same rule, read in the same order.
