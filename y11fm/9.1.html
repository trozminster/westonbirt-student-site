Multiplying Matrices

Every transformation in this chapter is a matrix multiplication, so the row-by-column rule you meet today is the machinery the whole of chapter 9 runs on - and the fact that AB is not BA is why order will matter when you combine transformations in 9.5

By the end you’ll know…
  • A matrix is an array of elements described by its order, rows x columns.
  • Two matrices can be multiplied only when the number of columns in the first equals the number of rows in the second, and the result takes the outer order (rows of the first x columns of the second).
  • Multiplying by a scalar multiplies every element; multiplying two matrices forms each entry of the product by pairing a row of the first with a column of the second and summing the products of corresponding entries.
  • Two matrices are equal only when every pair of corresponding elements is equal.

Which products exist

A matrix's order is rows × columns. Two matrices multiply only when the first's columns match the second's rows. The product takes the first's rows and the second's columns.

A matrix is described by its order, rows x columns, and two matrices can be multiplied only when the first has as many columns as the second has rows. The product then takes the rows of the first and the columns of the second.

Row by column

Each entry pairs one row of the first with one column of the second. Matching entries are multiplied, then added.

Each entry of a product pairs one row of the first matrix with one column of the second, and adds the products of the matching entries. The row decides the product's row and the column decides its column.

A scalar

Multiplying by a scalar scales every entry. The order of the matrix does not change.

Multiplying by a scalar scales every entry in the same way, so the order of the matrix does not change.

Equal matrices

Two matrices are equal only when every pair of corresponding entries is equal. One matrix equation therefore holds one ordinary equation for every position.

From a product to equations

A product equal to a known matrix gives one equation per entry. A correct solution satisfies all of them.

A product that equals a known matrix gives an equation for every entry of the product. A correct solution has to satisfy all of them.
