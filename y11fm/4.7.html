Algebraic Proof

A proof is an argument that holds for every case, not just the ones you tried, and learning to write one in algebra is how mathematics moves from "it seems to work" to "it must work".

By the end you’ll know…
  • A proof must hold for every case, so a general expression is required rather than examples: an even number is 2n, an odd number is 2n+1, and consecutive integers are n, n+1, n+2, ... for integer n.
  • A multiple of k is written as kn for integer n, and the argument must show the final expression is itself in that required form.
  • The final line should restate what has been proved.

Examples are not proof

Checking numbers can show a statement is false, never that it is always true. A proof uses letters to cover every case.

Checking numbers can show a statement is false, but it can never show it is true for all numbers. A proof uses letters, so that one argument covers every case.

General forms

Even, odd, consecutive and multiple all use a whole number n. Different numbers need different letters.

Even, odd, consecutive and multiple are all written with a whole number n, and different numbers need different letters. Every proof starts by choosing the right form.

The shape of a proof

A proof writes the general forms, then expands and simplifies. It shows the required form. The last line states what was proved.

A proof writes the general forms, expands and simplifies, shows the result is in the required form, and ends with a statement of what was proved. The last line is part of the proof.
WORKED EXAMPLE A
Prove that the sum of two consecutive integers is odd.
Consecutive integers are n and n+1, for any whole number n.
Let the integers be n and n+1
Add themn+(n+1) =2n+1
2n+1 is odd, so the sum of two consecutive integers is always odd
WORKED EXAMPLE B
Prove that the product of two even numbers is a multiple of 4
Two different even numbers need two different letters.
Let the numbers be 2n and 2m
Multiply2n×2m=4nm
4nm is 4 times an integer, so the product is always a multiple of 4

Proving an inequality

A quadratic is proved positive by completing the square. A square is never negative. So the expression is at least the constant.

To prove that a quadratic is always positive, complete the square, and then use the fact that a square is never negative. The smallest value of the square is zero, so the expression is at least the constant.

Proving an identity

An identity between fractions is proved by factorising and cancelling. Only a whole bracket cancels, never a single term.

To prove that two fractions are equal, factorise the top and bottom and then cancel the common factor. Only a whole bracket can be cancelled, never a single term.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Prove that x2−6x+11 is always positive.
Complete the square, then use that a square is never negative.
Complete the squarex2−6x+11 =(x−3)2+2
(x−3)2 is never negative, so the expression is at least 2
It is greater than 0 for every x, so it is always positive
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Prove that x2−4x2+5x+6 =x−2x+3.
Factorise the top and the bottom, then cancel the common bracket.
Factorise the topx2−4=(x−2)(x+2)
Factorise the bottomx2+5x+6=(x+2)(x+3)
Cancel the common factor (x+2)the fraction is x−2x+3

Hint. Follow the worked example: Complete the square

A square is at least 0, so the expression is at least 4, which is greater than 0
