Composite Functions

Real calculations come in chains, and a composite function is how a chain becomes one rule - but the order it is written in is the reverse of the order it is applied, and that is the point to get right.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In the composite fg(x) the inner function g is applied first.
  • Composition is different from adding, multiplying or dividing functions, and is not generally commutative.

The inner function acts first

In fg(x), the inner function g acts first. The output of g becomes the input of f.

In fg(x) the inner function g acts on x first and f acts on the result, so the function written next to x always goes first. The output of g becomes the input of f.

Order matters

Swapping the order of composition usually changes the answer. Here fg(2)=5 but gf(2)=6

Composition is not adding, multiplying or dividing two functions, and swapping the order usually gives a different function. Putting 2 into fg gives a different value from putting 2 into gf.

Combining is not composing

Adding two functions combines their outputs for the same input. Composing feeds the output of one into the other.

Adding, multiplying or dividing two functions combines their outputs for the same input, while composition feeds the output of one into the other. The two have different answers even for the same pair of functions.

Keep the brackets

Each function's whole expression goes in brackets. A product then needs expanding.

A sum, product or quotient of functions is written with each function's whole expression in brackets, so none of the terms is lost. The product of two binomials then needs expanding.
