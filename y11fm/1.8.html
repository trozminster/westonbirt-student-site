The Product Rule for Counting

Counting looks like the easiest thing in the chapter until the question adds that the first digit cannot be zero, or that order does not matter - and then the whole answer turns on which of those two you spotted, not on the arithmetic.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The product rule counts arrangements by multiplying the number of options at each stage.
  • Where order matters the count is a permutation, and where it does not, the internal arrangements must be divided out.
By the end you can
GFMApply the product rule to count arrangements, and evaluate factorial expressions.
GFMDistinguish arrangements where order matters from those where it does not, and count arrangements subject to positional or parity constraints.

Multiply the options

Independent choices multiply. So 3 shirts and 4 pairs of trousers give 3×4=12 outfits.

Factorials

Arranging n different objects in a line gives n! ways. That is n×(n−1) × ... × 1

WORKED EXAMPLE A
A PIN uses 4 digits from 0 to 9, and digits may repeat. How many PINs are there?
Each place has all 10 digits, because repeats are allowed.
Choices for each place10
10×10×10×10
10000 PINs

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The restricted place first

A place with a restriction is filled first. The other places then take what is left.

When order does not matter

An ordered count lists each group once for every order. Dividing by those orders counts each group once.

WORKED EXAMPLE C
How many 4-digit numbers can be made from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, with no repeats, if the number must be odd?
Fill the last place firstit must be 1, 3 or 5
Last digit odd3 ways
The other three places5×4×3=60
Total3×60=180
WORKED EXAMPLE D
A team of 3 is chosen from 7 people. How many teams are possible?
An ordered count lists each team 3! = 6 times.
Ordered7×6×5=210
Each team counted 3! = 6 times
Teams210÷6=35
YOUR TURN · c
How many 3-digit numbers, from 100 to 999, have three different digits?
The first digit has 9 choices (not 0), the second 9, and the third 8: 9×9×8=648
YOUR TURN · d
8 people each shake hands once with everyone else. How many handshakes are there?
8×7=56 ordered pairs, and each handshake is counted twice: 28
