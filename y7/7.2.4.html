Topic 7.2 · Sets and Venn Diagrams · Lesson 5 of 6

Building Venn Diagrams

So far your Venn diagrams have shown the prime factors of two numbers. Diagrams can also show any two or three sets - people who like football and people who like tennis, numbers that are prime and numbers that are odd. Today you learn to build the diagram itself: which regions exist, and where each element goes.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Each set is a closed region inside a rectangle representing the universal set.
  • Every element appears once, in the region matching all the sets it belongs to; elements in no set sit outside the circles but inside the rectangle.
  • Two sets make four regions; three sets make eight.
  • An Euler diagram shows only the relationships that occur, so it is used when sets share nothing or one lies inside another.
By the end you can
G4Populate a Venn diagram with elements of two or three sets, correctly placing shared elements in the intersection.

Two-set Venn diagrams

A two-set Venn diagram has two overlapping circles inside a rectangle. That makes four regions: only A, only B, both, and neither.

A two-set Venn diagram is a rectangle for ξ with two overlapping circles inside it, one for each set. That arrangement creates four regions: only in A, only in B, in both (the overlap), and in neither (inside the rectangle but outside both circles).

Placing elements

Here the sets are the factors of 12 and of 18
Check each element against both sets before placing it. An element in both sets goes in the overlap.

Check each element against both sets before placing it. An element that's in both sets goes in the overlap - putting it in only one circle is an error, since it belongs in both.

Three-set Venn diagrams

Three overlapping circles make eight regions. Check each element against all three sets.

Three overlapping circles inside a rectangle create eight regions: three 'only', three pairwise overlaps, one central region in all three, and one outside all three. Each element is checked against all three sets before being placed.

When to use an Euler diagram

An Euler diagram draws only the overlaps that happen. A Venn diagram shows every region, even empty ones.

An Euler diagram only draws the overlaps that actually happen - if two sets share nothing, their circles don't touch; if one set is entirely inside another, its circle is drawn inside the other's. Use a Venn diagram when every possible region needs to be shown (even empty ones); use an Euler diagram when only the relationships that actually occur need to be shown.

YOUR TURN · c
ξ ={1,...,30}, T = {multiples of 2},U = {multiples of 3},V = {multiples of 5}. Where do 12 and 1 belong?
12 is a multiple of 2 and of 3 but not of 5, so it goes in the overlap of T and U only. 1 is a multiple of none of them, so it sits outside all three circles.
YOUR TURN · d
W = {factors of 8} and X = {factors of 16}. Which shows them best, a Venn or an Euler diagram, and why?
W ={1,2,4,8} and X ={1,2,4,8,16}, and every element of W is in X, so W is a subset of X. An EULER diagram is the better choice: circle W is drawn inside circle X, which shows the subset directly, whereas a Venn would draw an overlap region and two empty ones.
