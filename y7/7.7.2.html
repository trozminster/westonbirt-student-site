Expanding Single Brackets

An expression like 3(x+4) is a single term times a bracket of two - and every term inside must be multiplied, not just the first one. Today's rule (the distributive property) is one of the most useful in all of algebra, and getting it right sets up simplifying and solving for the rest of the year.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Expanding a bracket multiplies every term inside by the term outside (the distributive property).
  • 3(x+4) =3x+12: the 3 reaches both terms, not just the first.
  • A negative outside the bracket changes the sign of every term inside: −2(x−5) =−2x+10
  • A minus sign alone in front of a bracket acts as −1
By the end you can

The distributive property

Expanding multiplies every term inside the bracket by the term outside. 3(x+4) =3x+12: the 3 reaches the x and the 4

Expanding a bracket multiplies every term inside by the term outside - not just the first one. 3(x+4) =3x+12: the 3 reaches both the x and the 4

An algebraic term outside the bracket

The same rule works with an algebraic term outside. 2x(3x+4) =6x2+8x: x times x gives x2

The same rule applies when the term outside is itself algebraic - every factor in the outside term multiplies every term inside, and any letter multiplying itself gains an index: 2x(3x+4) =6x2+8x.

A negative number outside the bracket

A negative outside changes the sign of every term inside. So −2(x−5) =−2x+10

A negative outside the bracket changes the sign of every term inside: −2(x−5) =−2x+10 - both the sign flip AND the multiplication happen to every term.

A lone minus sign

A lone minus in front of a bracket means multiply by −1
So −(x−5) =−x+5

A minus sign alone in front of a bracket acts as multiplying by −1, so −(x−5) =−x+5, changing the sign of every term inside exactly like −1(x−5) would.
