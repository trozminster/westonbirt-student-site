Expected and Experimental Probability

A factory tests a sample and a scientist repeats an experiment. Today you predict how often an outcome should happen, and use real results to judge whether something is fair.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The expected number of occurrences is the probability multiplied by the number of trials.
  • An expected value is a prediction rather than a guarantee, so real results vary around it.
  • Experimental probability (relative frequency) is the number of successes divided by the number of trials.
  • Experimental probability approaches theoretical probability as the number of trials increases.
  • A large gap between the two over many trials suggests the item is biased.

The expected number

Expected number = probability × number of trials. A fair dice rolled 60 times should give about 10 sixes.

The expected number of times an outcome happens is its probability multiplied by the number of trials. A fair dice rolled 60 times is expected to land on 6 about 16×60=10 times.

A prediction, not a promise

An expected number is a prediction. Real results vary around it: 8 or 13 sixes, not exactly 10

An expected number is a prediction, so real results vary around it. Sixty rolls might give 8 sixes or 13, rather than exactly 10

Experimental probability

Experimental probability is successes ÷ trials. It is also called relative frequency. 18 heads in 50 throws gives 18÷50=0.36

Experimental probability, or relative frequency, is the number of successes divided by the number of trials. 18 heads in 50 throws gives 18÷50=0.36

More trials, a better estimate

As the number of trials grows, experimental probability settles closer to the theoretical probability. A large gap over many trials suggests the item is biased.
