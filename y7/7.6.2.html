Topic 7.6 · Introducing Algebra · Lesson 2 of 5

Substituting Positive and Negative Values

A formula like A=lw is only useful once you can plug in real numbers and get a real answer - that's substitution. Today you learn to read algebraic shorthand back into arithmetic, first with positive numbers, then with negatives, where an easy mistake costs you the sign of your whole answer.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Substitution replaces each letter with a given value, then evaluates.
  • The conventions are undone as you substitute: 3a means 3×a, ab means a×b, a2 means a divided by 2
  • The order of operations still applies once the values are in.
  • A substituted negative keeps its sign inside brackets: with a=−4, 3a is 3×(−4)=−12 and 5−a is 5−(−4)=9

Undoing the shorthand

Substitution replaces each letter with its value, then evaluates. Never read 3a as digits side by side: it means 3×a.

Substitution replaces each letter with a given value, then evaluates. The algebraic conventions are undone as you substitute: 3a means 3×a, ab means a×b, a2 means a divided by 2 - none of these are ever read as digits written side by side.

Substituted negatives keep their sign

A substituted negative keeps its sign, inside brackets. With a=−4, 3a becomes 3×(−4)=−12

A substituted negative value keeps its sign, and is written inside brackets to keep it attached to the operation: with a=−4, 3a becomes 3×(−4)=−12

Subtracting a substituted negative

With a=−3, 5−a becomes 5−(−3)=8
Dropping the negative sign is the most common error here.

with a=−3, 5−a becomes 5−(−3), and subtracting a negative adds: 5−(−3) =5+3 =8
Dropping the negative sign here is the single most common error in this whole lesson.
