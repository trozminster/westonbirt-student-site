Complements and Mutually Exclusive Events

If there is a 30% chance of rain, what is the chance of no rain? Today you use the fact that all the probabilities add up to 1, and add the probabilities of events that cannot happen together.

By the end you’ll know…
  • The probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1
  • The complement of A is everything else, so P(not A)=1−P(A).
  • Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen at the same time.
  • The probability that one of several mutually exclusive events occurs is found by adding their probabilities.
By the end you can

Everything adds to 1

The probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to 1
So the probability that A does not happen is 1−P(A).

The complement in every form

Take a fraction or decimal complement from 1
Take a percentage complement from 100%. If P(A)=25, then P(not A)=35

For a fraction or a decimal the complement is taken from 1; for a percentage it is taken from 100%. If P(A)=25, then P(not A)=35, and 40% leaves 60%.

Cannot happen together

Mutually exclusive events cannot happen at the same time. Add their probabilities to find P(one or the other).

Mutually exclusive events cannot happen at the same time, such as rolling a 2 and rolling a 5 on one roll. The probability that one or the other happens is found by adding.

Events that overlap

Events that can happen together are not mutually exclusive. Their probabilities cannot simply be added. Both 4 and 6 are even and above 3

Events that can happen together are not mutually exclusive, so their probabilities cannot simply be added. An even number and a number above 3 can both happen, with a 4 or a 6
