Angles in Triangles

Today combines three things at once - the angle facts inside a triangle, a new way of describing direction (bearings), and combining everything you know about angles into multi-step "angle hunting" problems. It's a lot in one lesson, so the pace is brisk from the start.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Angles in a triangle sum to 180 degrees.
  • An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and three 60 degree angles; an isosceles triangle has two equal sides and the two angles opposite them are equal; a scalene triangle has none equal.
  • A right-angled triangle has one 90 degree angle, so its other two angles sum to 90.
  • A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from North, always written with three figures.
By the end you can
G4Calculate missing angles in triangles, including isosceles triangles, and measurestate simple bearings.
G5Find missing angles in multi-step diagrams (angle hunting), combining point, line and triangle facts with reasons given.

Triangle types and the angle sum

Angles in a triangle always sum to 180°. An isosceles triangle has equal angles opposite its two equal sides. An equilateral triangle has three 60° angles.

Angles in a triangle always sum to 180°. An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and three 60° angles; an isosceles triangle has two equal sides, and the two angles opposite those equal sides are also equal; a scalene triangle has no equal sides or angles. A right-angled triangle has one 90° angle, so its other two angles sum to 90.

Bearings

A bearing is measured clockwise from North. Write it with three figures: 045°, not 45.

A bearing is an angle measured clockwise from North, always written with three figures (e.g. 045°, not 45).

The two base angles are equal: (180 - 40) ÷ 2 = 140 ÷ 2 = 70°, so the other two angles are 70° and 70°.

Identifying the right relationship first

Several angle facts may apply in one diagram. Identify the right relationship before calculating.

In a multi-step diagram, several angle facts (around a point, on a line, vertically opposite, in a triangle) may apply in different parts of the same diagram. The relationship must be identified correctly BEFORE calculating - using the first fact that seems to fit, without checking, is the main source of error.

Working through step by step, with reasons

Give the angle fact as the reason for each step. Reasons let an error be checked and caught.

Each step of an angle-hunting problem should state which angle fact was used as a reason (e.g. "angles on a straight line," "angles in a triangle"), not just the final number - this is what lets an error be checked and caught.

Missing angle= 50°
Vertically opposite angles are equalz = 50°
YOUR TURN · c
Find z, giving the angle fact for each step.
The third angle is 180 - 55 - 71 = 54° (angles in a triangle add up to 180°). z is vertically opposite it, so z = 54°.
YOUR TURN · d
Find the apex angle of this isosceles triangle, then the two base angles.
The apex angle is 180 - 110 = 70° (angles on a straight line). The base angles are equal: (180 - 70) ÷ 2 = 55° each.
