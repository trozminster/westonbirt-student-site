Constructing Triangles

You've used arcs to bisect lines and angles, and to build loci and regions. Today the same skill - equal-radius arcs crossing exactly - constructs a whole shape: a triangle, built from nothing but three lengths.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Three side lengths fix a triangle completely (SSS): the third vertex is where two arcs cross.
  • The arcs cross twice, giving mirror images of the same triangle.
  • A triangle exists only if the two shorter sides add to more than the longest.
  • Equal arc radii construct an isosceles triangle.
By the end you can
G4Construct a triangle accurately given three side lengths (SSS) using intersecting arcs.
G5Solve loci and construction problems in context, selecting the correct construction technique(s).

SSS construction

Three side lengths fix a triangle. Draw the longest as the base. Arcs of the other two lengths, from each end, cross at the third vertex.

Three side lengths fix a triangle completely, so the construction starts from the longest of them, drawn as a base line. Set compasses to the second length and draw an arc from one end; set to the third length and draw an arc from the other end. Where the two arcs cross is the third vertex.

Two crossings, one triangle

The arcs cross twice, making two mirror-image triangles: use either. If the two shorter sides do not exceed the longest, the arcs never meet.

The two arcs actually cross twice (once above the base, once below) - both crossings give a valid triangle, and they are mirror images (congruent) of each other, so either can be used. A triangle only exists if the two shorter sides add to more than the longest - otherwise the arcs never meet.

Choosing the right technique

The words describe a situation, not the construction. Equidistant means a bisector. A fixed distance means a circle or parallel lines.

A construction problem in context describes a real situation, not the technique by name - the words tell you which construction(s) apply. "Equidistant from two points" needs a perpendicular bisector. "Equidistant from two lines/arms of an angle" needs an angle bisector. "A fixed distance from a point" needs a circle (locus from a point). "A fixed distance from a line" needs a pair of parallel lines (a racetrack round a line segment). "Fixed side lengths" needs SSS. Some contexts need more than one technique combined, with the region satisfying every condition together being the answer.

Working through a multi-technique problem

Construct each condition separately, with its own technique. Then combine: the answer satisfies every condition at once.

identify every condition in the problem separately, construct each one in turn using its own correct technique, then combine - for a region, that means the space satisfying ALL conditions at once (the overlap); for a single point or line, checking each construction lands on the same answer. Order doesn't usually matter, but constructing one condition at a time (never guessing at the combined answer directly) keeps the working checkable.
WORKED EXAMPLE C

Whyevery point on it is the same distance from both
