Adding and Subtracting Fractions

Half a cup of milk and a third of a cup of water - how much liquid is that? Halves and thirds are different-sized pieces, so today is about cutting them into pieces that match before combining them.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Fractions can only be added or subtracted when they share a denominator, since only equal-sized parts combine.
  • A common denominator is any common multiple of the denominators, and the LCM gives the smallest.
  • Where one denominator is a multiple of the other, only one fraction needs changing.
  • Where they share no factor, multiplying the denominators always gives a common one.
  • Only the numerators are added; the denominator names the part size and stays the same.
By the end you can
G3Add and subtract fractions with related denominators, where one denominator is a multiple of the other.
G4Add and subtract fractions with different denominators, both where the denominators share a common factor and where they do not.

Only equal parts combine

Only fractions with same-size parts can be added or subtracted. Combine the numerators and keep the denominator.

Fractions can be added or subtracted only when their parts are the same size. The numerators are combined and the denominator stays, because it names the size of the parts.

One denominator a multiple of the other

When one denominator is a multiple of the other, only one fraction needs rewriting. The smaller denominator is scaled up to the larger.

Learn

Rewriting both fractions

When neither denominator is a multiple of the other, rewrite both. The LCM keeps the numbers smallest.

When neither denominator is a multiple of the other, both fractions are rewritten. Any common multiple works, and the LCM keeps the numbers smallest.

When the denominators share a factor

Where the denominators share no factor, their product is the LCM. Where they do share one, the LCM is smaller than the product.
