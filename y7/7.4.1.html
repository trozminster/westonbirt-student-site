Proof by Counter-Example

In maths, one example is never proof that something is ALWAYS true - but it only takes one example to prove something is NOT always true. Today's skill is finding that one example: a counter-example.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A counter-example is one case meeting a statement's conditions but not its conclusion, and it proves the statement false.
  • One counter-example disproves a statement; no number of supporting examples proves one true.
  • Examples can only disprove an 'always' claim or prove a 'sometimes' claim; 'always' and 'never' need an argument covering every case.
  • Counter-examples usually sit at the edges: 0, 1, 2, negatives, fractions, and the number itself.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

What a counter-example does

A counter-example meets a statement's conditions but fails its conclusion. Just one disproves the whole statement. No number of supporting examples can prove an 'always' statement.

A counter-example is a single case that meets a statement's conditions but not its conclusion. Finding just one disproves the whole statement - no number of supporting examples can prove an 'always' statement true, since the very next case might be the counter-example.

Always, sometimes, never

Examples can show a statement is sometimes true. Always and never need an argument covering every case. Counter-examples hide at the edges: 0, 1, 2, negatives and fractions.

A statement can be tested by trying examples systematically. If every example works and there's a reason covering every case, it's always true. If some examples work and some don't, it's sometimes true. If no example works, it's never true - but 'always' and 'never' really need an argument covering every case, since examples alone can't check infinitely many numbers. Counter-examples usually hide at the edges: 0, 1, 2, negatives, and fractions.

Is the statement 'the sum of two odd numbers is even' always, sometimes, or never true? Test with 3+5, 7+9, and 1+1.
Test the first3 + 5 = 8

YOUR TURN · a
Is the statement 'squaring a number always makes it bigger' always, sometimes, or never true? Try n = 3, 1, 0.5 and -2.
SOMETIMES true: 3 squared = 9; 1 squared = 1; 0.5 squared = 0.25; -2 squared = 4. It holds for 3 and for -2, but 1 squared stays 1 and 0.5 squared gets SMALLER, so the statement is not always true.
YOUR TURN · b
Find a counter-example to disprove: 'the difference between two square numbers is always odd.'
3² - 1² = 9 - 1 = 8, which is even - one counter-example is enough to disprove the statement.
