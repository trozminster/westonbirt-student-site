Standard Form: Small Numbers

The same standard form idea that writes very large numbers compactly also handles the very small - a negative index means the number gets smaller, not negative, which is where most of today's mistakes live.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A negative index means a reciprocal: 10⁻ⁿ = 110ⁿ.
  • So multiplying by 10⁻ⁿ divides, shifting the digits n places to the right.
  • A negative index in standard form therefore represents a number smaller than 1.
  • The index counts place shifts, not zeros, so 4.2 × 10⁻³ = 0.0042.
  • A stays between 1 and 10 whatever the sign of the index.

Negative indices

A negative index is a reciprocal: 10⁻ⁿ = 110ⁿ. It shifts the digits right, not left. The value stays positive.

A negative index is a reciprocal: 10⁻ⁿ means 110ⁿ, so multiplying by it divides and shifts every digit n places to the right. The value stays positive, and A still sits between 1 and 10 whatever the sign of the index.

Four conversions, one skill

Converting either way is one skill. The sign of the index sets the direction of the shift. Then count the places.

Converting fluently between standard form and ordinary form, in both directions and with either sign of index, is the same single skill applied four ways - decide the direction of the shift from the sign, then count the places.
