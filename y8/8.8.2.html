Topic 8.8 · Area and Circumference of Circles · Lesson 2 of 4

Circumference Problems

A car's speedometer doesn't measure distance directly - it counts wheel revolutions and uses the tyre's circumference to convert that into distance travelled. Today you work circumference problems backwards, finding a circle's size from its circumference, then forwards into real contexts like wheels and paths.

By the end you’ll know…
  • π is the ratio of any circle's circumference to its diameter, and is the same for every circle.
  • So C=π×d, which is the same as C=2×π×r.
  • The diameter is found from the circumference by dividing by π, and the radius by dividing by 2×π.
  • Leaving an answer in terms of π keeps it exact, whereas a decimal answer has been rounded.
  • One full revolution of a wheel covers one circumference.
By the end you can
G5Find the radius or diameter given the circumference, and give answers exactly in terms of π.
G5Solve circumference problems in context, such as wheel revolutions and perimeters of shapes with circular edges.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

What π is

π is any circle's circumference ÷ its diameter. It is the same for every circle, however large or small.

π is the ratio of any circle's circumference to its diameter, and this ratio is the same for every circle, however large or small.

Running the formula backwards

To find the diameter, divide the circumference by π. To find the radius, divide by 2×π.

Since C=π×d, finding the diameter from a known circumference means dividing by π: d=C÷π. Since the diameter is twice the radius, finding the radius from the circumference means dividing by 2×π instead: r=C÷(2×π).

Exact answers and rounded ones

Leaving an answer as a multiple of π keeps it exact. A decimal answer has been rounded. Don't round early when the question asks for exact.

Leaving an answer as a multiple of π keeps it exact; only a decimal answer has been rounded, and a question that asks for an exact answer must not be rounded early.

Learn

One revolution is one circumference

One full turn of a wheel covers one circumference. Multiply by the number of turns for the total distance.

One full revolution of a wheel covers exactly one circumference - so a wheel's circumference, multiplied by the number of revolutions, gives the total distance travelled.

Perimeters that are partly curved

Only the curved edge uses the circumference formula. Measure the straight edges and add them on.

In a perimeter made partly from a circular edge and partly from straight edges, only the circular part uses the circumference formula; the straight edges are measured and added separately, exactly as with any other compound perimeter.

Explain why dividing a circumference by 2×π (rather than just π) gives the radius instead of the diameter.
Circumference = π × diameter = 2×π × radius, so dividing by π alone gives the diameter, while dividing by 2×π gives the radius directly.
