Topic 8.4 · Similarity and Congruence · Lesson 3 of 3

Congruent Triangles

Two triangles can look identical without a checklist proving it - mathematicians have found exactly four shortcuts that guarantee two triangles are truly congruent (same size and shape), plus two tempting shortcuts that don't actually work. Today you'll learn to spot which real condition applies, and use it to prove things about a diagram are exactly equal.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Congruent shapes are identical in both size and shape, whereas similar shapes share angles but not size.
  • Two triangles are congruent if they satisfy SSS (three sides), SAS (two sides and the angle between them), ASA (two angles and a corresponding side) or RHS (right angle, hypotenuse and one other side).
  • AAA proves similarity only, because equal angles fix a shape's proportions but not its size.
  • SSA is not a condition, since two different triangles can share two sides and a non-included angle.
  • Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal, which is what makes congruence useful for proving two lengths or angles equal.

Learn

Congruent and similar

Congruent shapes are identical: equal lengths and equal angles. Similar shapes have equal angles, with sides in the same ratio. A ratio of 1 means congruent.

Congruent shapes are identical in both size and shape - every corresponding length AND angle is equal. Similar shapes share equal corresponding angles but not necessarily equal size - corresponding sides are in the same ratio, which could be a ratio of 1 (congruent) or any other value.

The four conditions

Four conditions prove triangles congruent: SSS, SAS, ASA and RHS. In SAS, the angle must sit between the two sides.

Four conditions guarantee two triangles are congruent: SSS (all three sides match); SAS (two sides AND the angle BETWEEN them match); ASA (two angles and the side between them match); RHS (right angle, hypotenuse and one other side match, for right-angled triangles). To decide which applies, check exactly what information is given and match it to one of these four patterns.

Two that look valid and are not

AAA only proves similarity: equal angles do not fix the size. SSA fails: two different triangles can share those parts.

Two patterns that look tempting but DON'T work: AAA only proves similarity, because equal angles fix a shape's proportions but not its size. SSA (two sides and a NON-included angle) is not a valid condition, because two different triangles can be built from the same two sides and non-included angle.
TWO SIDES AND AN ANGLE
Model: Triangle ABC has AB=5 cm, BC=7 cm, and angle ABC=40° (between AB and BC). Triangle DEF has DE=5 cm, EF=7 cm, and angle DEF=40° (between DE and EF). Step 1: List what's given for each triangle - two sides and an angle, for both. Step 2: Check whether the angle is INCLUDED (between the two known sides) or not - angle ABC is between AB and BC, and angle DEF is between DE and EF, so both angles ARE included. Step 3: Two sides plus the included angle matches SAS. Step 4: State the condition: the triangles are congruent by SAS.
The 40° angle is between the two known sidesincluded
Two sides and the included anglecongruent by SAS

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Every corresponding part is equal

Once triangles are congruent, every corresponding part is equal. That proves sides or angles equal that you never measured.

Once you know two triangles are congruent (by SSS, SAS, ASA or RHS), every corresponding part is equal - not just the parts you were given, but every other side and angle too. This is what makes congruence useful for proving results: if you can show two triangles are congruent, you can conclude that a pair of sides or angles you haven't directly measured must be equal, because they are corresponding parts of congruent triangles.

Naming the condition

A congruence proof ends by naming the condition used. Listing the equal parts alone is not a proof.

A full proof always ends by stating which condition was used - identifying the matching parts alone does not complete a congruence proof.
A CONGRUENCE PROOF
Model: Triangle ABC is isosceles with AB=AC, and D is the midpoint of BC, with AD drawn.
Prove that angle ABD = angle ACD.
Identify the two triangles to comparetriangle ABD and triangle ACD.
List what's knownAB=AC (given)
BD=DC (D is the midpoint of BC)
AD=AD (shared side, common to both triangles).
Step 3: This is three matching pairs of sides - SSS.
State the conditiontriangle ABD is congruent to triangle ACD (SSS).
Step 5: Because the triangles are congruent, all corresponding parts are equal, so angle ABD = angle ACD - which is what we needed to prove.

YOUR TURN · d
Prove that PT=RT, given PQ=SR.
Hint. what do the parallel lines give you?
in triangles PQT and RST: angle QPT = angle SRT and angle PQT = angle RST (alternate angles, PQ parallel to SR, on the transversals PR and QS), and PQ=SR (given). That is two angles with the side BETWEEN them, so the triangles are congruent by ASA, and PT=RT because corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.

1
Explain the difference between similar shapes and congruent shapes, and explain why AAA (three matching angles) is not a valid condition for proving triangles congruent.
Similar shapes have the same angles; congruent shapes are also the same size. AAA fails because a scaled copy has the same angles.
2
Triangle FGH has FG=7 cm, GH=9 cm, and angle FGH=48° (between FG and GH). Triangle JKL has JK=7 cm, KL=9 cm, and angle JKL=48° (between JK and KL). State the condition that proves these triangles are congruent.
SAS (two sides and the included angle match).
3
Triangle STU and triangle SVU share the side SU. Angle STU = angle SVU=90°, and ST=SV. Prove that triangle STU is congruent to triangle SVU, stating the condition used, and use this to explain why angle TSU = angle VSU.
SU is common, ST=SV and both angles are 90°, so congruent by RHS. So angle TSU = angle VSU (corresponding angles).
