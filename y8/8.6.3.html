Changing the Subject

A formula like A=s2 for a square's area works both ways: substitute a value for s to find A, or rearrange it to make s the subject and find any square's side straight from its area. Today you'll do both - starting with substituting into one-step power and root formulae, then learning the algebra that flips a formula around.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Changing the subject rearranges a formula so that a different variable stands alone.
  • It uses the same inverse operations as solving an equation, but the result is a formula rather than a number.
  • The operations are undone in the reverse of the order they were applied to the variable wanted as subject.
  • Squaring and rooting are inverses, so a squared subject is freed by rooting both sides.
  • A rearranged formula answers for any values, whereas substituting first answers for only one set.

Substituting into a formula

a formula relates two or more variables, with one written as the subject. Substituting a known value for every OTHER variable leaves a numerical calculation for the subject.

Powers and roots in formulae

A power or root acts only on the variable it is attached to. Brackets can make it reach the whole expression.

where the formula contains a power or a root, that operation is simply carried out on the substituted value, in the correct order - the power or root applies only to the variable it's attached to, not to the whole expression, unless brackets say otherwise.

Freeing a squared subject

To free a squared subject, square root both complete sides. Keep both roots: s=±A. Context, like a length, may rule out the negative.

if the subject is trapped inside a square (e.g. A=s2), it is freed by taking the square root of both complete sides. A square root has both a positive and a negative value, so the general rearranged formula is s=±A - context (such as s being a length) may then rule out the negative.

Freeing a rooted subject

To free a rooted subject, square both complete sides. Squaring and rooting are inverse operations.

if the subject is trapped under a root (e.g. T=h), it is freed by squaring both complete sides: T2=h. Squaring removes a root exactly as rooting removes a square - they are inverse operations, applied to BOTH complete sides, never to just one.

A coefficient alongside the power

Undo a coefficient FIRST, to isolate the power term. Then root or square the whole expression together.

where the power or root term also carries a coefficient (e.g. A=4r2), that coefficient must be undone FIRST, isolating the power or root term, before the root or power itself is applied - the whole expression is rooted or squared together, not just part of it.
UNDOING A SQUARE

Write the formulaA=4r2
Divide both sides by 4A4=r2(isolate the power term first)
Square root both sides±A4=r
Swap the sidesr=±A4
A radius is positive, so in practice only the positive root is used, but the general rearranged formula keeps both
