Topic 8.17 · Averages from Frequency Tables · Lesson 4 of 4

Grouped Continuous Data

Times, heights and masses are never exactly whole numbers, so their tables need classes with no gaps. And when one result changes, a mean can be updated without starting again.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Continuous data can take any value in a range, so its classes are written as inequalities rather than separate values.
  • Inequality boundaries leave no gaps, so every possible value falls into exactly one class.
  • The midpoint of a class is halfway between its two boundaries.
  • Because the total is mean x count, the original total can always be recovered from a given mean.
  • A value added, removed or corrected changes that total and possibly the count, and the new mean follows from both.

Classes with no gaps

Continuous data can take any value. So classes are inequalities: 10 < t ≤ 20. No gaps: every value falls in exactly one class.

Continuous data can take any value, so its classes are written as inequalities such as 10 < t ≤ 20. The boundaries leave no gaps, so every value falls into exactly one class.

The midpoint of a continuous class

The midpoint is halfway between the two boundaries. For 10 < t ≤ 20 it is 15. The estimated mean then works as for grouped discrete data.

The total hides inside the mean

The total is the mean × the count. So the total can always be recovered from a mean. A changed total gives a new mean: new total ÷ new count.

Because the total is the mean × the count, the total can always be recovered from a mean. A change to the data changes the total, and the new mean is the new total ÷ the new count.

Removed or corrected

Removing a value lowers both the total and the count. Correcting a value leaves the count alone. Take off the wrong value and add the right one.

Removing a value lowers both the total and the count. Correcting a value leaves the count alone: take off the wrong value and add the right one.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Find the new mean when a seventh score of 22 joins 6 scores with mean 15.
Multiply for the total6 × 15 = 90
Add the new score90 + 22 = 112
Divide by 7 scores112 ÷ 7 = 16
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Find the true mean of 5 heights, mean 150 cm, when one recorded as 142 cm is really 162 cm.
Multiply for the total5 × 150 = 750 cm
Subtract the wrong height750 - 142 = 608 cm
Add the true height608 + 162 = 770 cm
Divide by 5 heights770 ÷ 5 = 154 cm
YOUR TURN · c
The mean of 8 numbers is 10. The number 3 is removed. Find the new mean.
80 - 3 = 77, over 7 numbers: 77 ÷ 7 = 11.
YOUR TURN · d
4 boys have a mean mass of 50 kg and 6 girls a mean of 45 kg. Find the mean of all 10.
200 + 270 = 470, and 470 ÷ 10 = 47 kg.

The mean of 4 numbers is 9. A fifth number, 14, is added. What is the new mean?
Not quite. Don't add the value to the mean. Find the total: 4 × 9 = 36.
Not quite. Averaging the mean with the value ignores the count. 36 + 14 = 50, then ÷ 5.

Yes. 4 × 9 = 36, 36 + 14 = 50, and 50 ÷ 5 = 10.

Not quite. There are now 4 values: 70 ÷ 4.

Not quite. Take 30 off the total, 100, not off the mean.
