Topic 8.16 · Congruence and Constructions · Lesson 1 of 7

Compasses and Perpendicular Bisectors

A pair of compasses and a straight edge can find exact points and lines with no measuring at all. Today we find the line that cuts a segment exactly in half, and see why it works.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A locus is the set of all points satisfying a given rule.
  • Equidistant means the same distance from.
  • A circle is the locus of points a fixed distance (the radius) from a point, which is what a pair of compasses draws.
  • Constructions therefore work by geometrical property, never by measurement with a ruler or protractor.
  • Two intersecting arcs locate the one point that is a set distance from each of two others.
By the end you can
G4Use compasses accurately to construct arcs, drawing arcs rather than full circles for efficiency.
G5Construct the perpendicular bisector of a line segment, identifying the rhombus that the construction creates.

A locus is a set of points

A locus is every point that obeys a rule. Equidistant means the same distance from. Compasses draw all points one fixed distance from a point: a circle.

A locus is every point that obeys a rule, and equidistant means the same distance from. Compasses draw the locus of points one fixed distance from a point: a circle.

Two distances fix a point

A point 5 cm from A and 4 cm from B lies on two circles. So it is where they cross.

A point 5 cm from A lies on one circle, and a point 4 cm from B on another. It must lie on both, so it is where the two circles cross.

Arcs, not circles

A construction draws only a short arc of each circle. Two distances fix the point. Never measure where it looks right.

Only the part of each circle near the crossing is needed, so a construction draws two short arcs. The point is fixed by the two distances, never by measuring where it looks right.

Learn: The perpendicular bisector

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The perpendicular bisector

One compass width, more than half of AB, draws arcs from A and B. They cross at P and Q. PQ is the perpendicular bisector: APBQ is a rhombus.

One compass width, more than half of AB, draws arcs from A and then from B, crossing at P and Q. The line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB, since APBQ is a rhombus and its diagonals bisect each other at right angles. The width stays fixed for the whole construction. Every point on an arc from A is that one width from A, so P and Q are each exactly as far from A as from B. APBQ has four sides equal to the one compass width.
