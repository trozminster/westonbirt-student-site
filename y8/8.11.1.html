Topic 8.11 · Pythagoras' Theorem · Lesson 1 of 4

Finding the Hypotenuse

Builders once made perfect right angles with a rope knotted into lengths of 3, 4 and 5
Pythagoras' theorem explains why that works, and it finds the longest side of any right-angled triangle.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In a right-angled triangle, a2+b2=c2
  • The hypotenuse c is the longest side and always lies opposite the right angle.
  • The theorem holds only for right-angled triangles.
  • The hypotenuse is found by adding the squares of the two shorter sides and taking the square root.
  • A Pythagorean triple is a set of three whole numbers fitting the theorem: 3−4−5, 5−12−13, 8−15−17, and any multiple of these.

The squares on the sides

In a right-angled triangle, a2+b2=c2
The hypotenuse c squared is the sum of the other two squares.

In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides. This is written a2+b2=c2, where c is the hypotenuse.

The hypotenuse

The hypotenuse is the longest side and always lies opposite the right angle. The theorem holds only for right-angled triangles.

Pythagorean triples

Three whole numbers that fit a2+b2=c2 form a Pythagorean triple. 3, 4, 5 and 5, 12, 13 are triples. So is any multiple of one.

A Pythagorean triple is three whole numbers that fit a2+b2=c2, such as 3, 4, 5 and 5, 12, 13
Any multiple of a triple is also a triple.

A triple saves the calculation

If two sides match a triple, the third side follows. No squaring is needed. Check with a2+b2=c2

When two sides match a triple, or a multiple of one, the third side follows without any squaring. Checking that a2+b2=c2 confirms it.
