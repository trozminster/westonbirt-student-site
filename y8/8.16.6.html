Topic 8.16 · Congruence and Constructions · Lesson 6 of 7

Constructing Triangles and Circles

Every triangle has one circle through its corners and one that fits snugly inside it, and a pair of compasses can find both. First, we see which facts pin a triangle down, and which let it wobble.

By the end you’ll know…
  • Three sides (SSS), two sides and the angle between them (SAS), or two angles and the side between them (ASA) each fix a triangle uniquely.
  • SSA does not: two sides and a non-included angle can produce two different triangles, which is the ambiguous case.
  • The incircle touches all three sides, so its centre is where the three angle bisectors meet.
  • The circumcircle passes through all three vertices, so its centre is where the three perpendicular bisectors meet.
  • Both follow from the loci: equidistant from the sides gives the incircle, equidistant from the vertices the circumcircle.

What fixes a triangle

SSS, SAS and ASA each fix one triangle. Every accurate construction from those facts gives the same triangle.

Three sides (SSS), two sides and the angle between them (SAS), or two angles and the side between them (ASA) fix one triangle. Every accurate construction from those facts gives the same triangle.

The ambiguous case

With SSA, the arc for the third side can cut the base line twice. Both crossings give a triangle with the given parts. So SSA can give two answers.

With two sides and an angle not between them (SSA), the arc for the third side can cut the base line twice. Both crossings give a triangle with the given parts, so SSA can give two answers.

05

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The circumcircle

The three perpendicular bisectors meet at O, equidistant from A, B and C. So the circle centre O through A passes through B and C too.

A point equidistant from two vertices lies on their perpendicular bisector, so the three perpendicular bisectors meet at one point, O. O is the same distance from all three vertices, so the circle with centre O through A passes through B and C too.

The incircle

The three angle bisectors meet at I, the same perpendicular distance from each side. So the circle with centre I and that radius touches each side once.

A point equidistant from two sides lies on the bisector of the angle between them, so the three angle bisectors meet at one point, I. I is the same perpendicular distance from all three sides, so the circle with centre I and that radius touches each side once.
WORKED EXAMPLE C
Construct the circumcircle of triangle ABC.
Bisectthe perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC
Centrewhere they meet, O
Circleradius OA, which passes through B and C
WORKED EXAMPLE D
Construct the incircle of triangle PQR.
Bisectthe angles at P and at Q
Centrewhere the bisectors meet, I
Radiusthe perpendicular from I to PQ
Circlecentre I, touching all three sides
YOUR TURN · c
Draw a triangle and construct its circumcircle. Check that it passes through all three vertices.
Two perpendicular bisectors meet at the centre; its distance to each vertex is the radius.
YOUR TURN · d
The perpendicular bisectors of an obtuse triangle meet outside it. Has the construction gone wrong?
No: an obtuse triangle always has its circumcentre outside it.

Exit ticket

1
Why does the centre of the circumcircle lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB?
It is equidistant from A and B, and that locus is the perpendicular bisector of AB.
2
Construct triangle ABC with AB=6 cm, angle A=70° and angle B=45°.
Base AB; 70° at A and 45° at B; C is where the two arms meet (ASA).

Construct the incircle of a triangle. What sets its radius?
Two angle bisectors meet at the centre; the radius is the perpendicular distance from the centre to a side.
