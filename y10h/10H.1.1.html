Using nth Terms

An nth term jumps straight to any term of a sequence, the 100th as easily as the 3rd. Read backwards, it tells you whether a number belongs to the sequence at all.

By the end you’ll know…
  • A term-to-term rule gives the next term from the previous one; a position-to-term rule, the nth term, gives any term directly from its position n.
  • An arithmetic sequence changes by the same amount each time, its common difference, found by subtracting any term from the one after it; a negative common difference means the sequence decreases.
  • Substituting n=1, 2, 3, ... into the nth term generates the sequence: 4n−1 gives 3, 7, 11, 15
  • A number is a term only if setting the nth term equal to it and solving gives a positive whole number n, and that n is its position.

Two kinds of rule

A term-to-term rule needs the term before. The nth term jumps straight to any position.

Position in, term out

The position n goes into the nth term. The term comes out.

A negative difference

A rule with −3n falls by 3 at every step. Its terms decrease.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Read the rule backwards

Setting the nth term equal to a number makes an equation in n. Solving it gives the number's position.

Positions are whole numbers

A position must be a positive whole number. Any other answer means the number is not a term.

The number is the term

The number tested goes where the term is, never in place of n. Substituting it answers a different question.

A TERM
Is 85 a term of the sequence with nth term 6n+7?
Set the rule equal6n+7=85
Subtract 7 from both sides6n=78
Position of 85n=13, the 13th term
NOT A TERM
Now is 100 a term of the same sequence?
Set the rule equal6n+7=100
Subtract 7 from both sides6n=93
Not a whole numbern=15.5, so not a term
