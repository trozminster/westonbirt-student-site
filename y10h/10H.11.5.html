Harder Recurring Decimals

Some decimals wait before they repeat, like 0.1666... for one sixth. Two multiples of x, not one, make the recurring parts cancel.

By the end you’ll know…
  • When digits that do not recur come before the repeating block, two multiples of x are needed: one power of ten moves the point to the start of the block, and another moves it to the end of the first block.
  • Subtracting the smaller from the larger removes the recurring part, so for x=0.1666..., 100x−10x =16.666...−1.666..., giving 90x=15 and x=16
  • The denominator before simplifying is one 9 for each recurring digit followed by one 0 for each non-recurring digit after the point.
  • A 'show that' answer writes both equations, the subtraction and the simplifying, ending at the fraction given.
  • Recurring decimals are added, subtracted, multiplied or divided exactly by writing each as a fraction first.
By the end you can

Two shifts when a digit comes first

A digit before the block needs two shifts. One reaches the block's start, and one its end.

When a digit comes before the block, one power of ten moves the point to the start of the block. Another moves it to the end of the first block.

Nines, then zeros

One 9 per recurring digit, then one 0 per digit before the block. So 0.2454545... gives 990

The denominator has one 9 for each recurring digit, then one 0 for each digit before the block. So 0.2454545... gives a denominator of 990 before simplifying.

A proof starts from the decimal

A 'show that' starts from the decimal. It writes every step and ends at the given fraction.

A 'show that' starts from the decimal and ends at the fraction given. Writing every equation and the subtraction is what proves it.

Exact arithmetic uses fractions

Recurring decimals never end, so their digits can't add exactly. As fractions, they can.

Recurring decimals never end, so adding their digits cannot give an exact answer. Writing each one as a fraction first gives the exact result.
