Linear Graphs in Context

Phone contracts, taxi fares and a draining bath can all be modelled by straight lines. The gradient is the rate, and the intercept is the starting value.

By the end you’ll know…
  • In a real context both axes carry units, so the gradient carries units too: y units per x unit.
  • On a distance-time graph the gradient is the speed.
  • The intercept is the value when the x variable is zero, such as a fixed charge before any usage.
  • A straight line means a constant rate of change, which is what makes the model linear.
By the end you can
G6Interpret linear graphs in context, explaining what the gradient and intercept represent.
G6Find the equation of a linear model from two values in context, and use it to predict, saying whether the prediction can be trusted.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

The gradient is a rate

The gradient is the y quantity per unit of the x quantity. Its units come from the two axes.

The intercept is the starting value

The intercept is the value of y when x is zero. On a cost graph it is a fixed charge.

Read the scales, not the squares

The two axes may have different scales. So the gradient comes from values, not from squares.

Learn

Read each card, then follow the worked examples one step at a time.

Two values fix the model

Two data points give the rate as a gradient. The model then follows as y=mx+c.

The intercept may lie off the data

The data need not start at zero. So c comes from the model, not from the first value.

Predict only inside the data

A model is trusted within the range of its data. Far outside it, the pattern may change.

COST FROM TWO BILLS
